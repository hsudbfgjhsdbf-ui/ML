## Chapter 1: Introduction and Problem Statement    
    
### 1.1 Background    
    
Medical insurance fraud is one of the most pervasive and financially damaging problems in the healthcare industry globally. Every year, billions of dollars are lost to fraudulent insurance claims that range from billing for services never rendered, inflating the cost of medical procedures, performing unnecessary surgeries or treatments solely for financial gain, to identity theft where someone else's insurance details are used to obtain medical services. In the United States alone, the National Health Care Anti-Fraud Association estimates that healthcare fraud costs the nation approximately tens of billions of dollars each year. The situation is no different in India and other developing nations where the insurance sector is growing rapidly but regulatory oversight and fraud detection mechanisms have not kept pace with the increasing volume of claims.    
    
The traditional method of detecting fraud relies heavily on manual auditing. Insurance companies hire teams of investigators and auditors who review claims one by one, looking for anomalies, inconsistencies, and red flags. This process is not only extraordinarily time-consuming and expensive but also prone to human error. An auditor can only review a limited number of claims per day, and the sheer volume of claims submitted to large insurance companies means that a significant percentage of fraudulent claims inevitably slip through undetected. Moreover, fraudsters are becoming increasingly sophisticated in their methods, making it even harder for human auditors to identify fraudulent patterns without the aid of technology.    
    
This is precisely where machine learning and data science come into play. By training classification algorithms on historical data that contains both legitimate and fraudulent claims, we can build models that automatically learn the patterns, correlations, and anomalies that distinguish fraudulent claims from legitimate ones. Once trained, these models can process thousands or even millions of claims in a matter of seconds, flagging suspicious claims for further human review. This dramatically reduces the time and cost associated with fraud detection while simultaneously improving accuracy.    
    
### 1.2 Problem Statement    
    
The core problem we are addressing in this project is as follows: Given a dataset of medical insurance claims where each claim is labeled as either fraudulent or legitimate, can we build a machine learning classification model that accurately predicts whether a new, unseen insurance claim is fraudulent or not? In simpler terms, when a user comes forward to claim their medical insurance, our system should be able to analyze the details of the claim and output a binary decision — YES, this claim is likely fraudulent, or NO, this claim appears to be legitimate.    
    
This is fundamentally a binary classification task. The target variable has two possible values: fraud (labeled as 1 or YES) and not fraud (labeled as 0 or NO). The input features consist of various attributes associated with the insurance claim, the policyholder, the healthcare provider, and the medical procedures involved.

## Chapter 2: Literature Review and Related Work    
    
### 2.1 Overview of Medical Insurance Fraud    
    
Medical insurance fraud is defined as the intentional deception or misrepresentation by an individual or entity that results in unauthorized financial gain from a medical insurance program. The types of medical insurance fraud are varied and include but are not limited to the following categories.    
    
Provider fraud occurs when healthcare providers such as doctors, hospitals, clinics, or laboratories submit false claims to insurance companies. This can take the form of billing for services that were never provided (phantom billing), billing for a more expensive procedure than what was actually performed (upcoding), unbundling services that should be billed together to inflate the total cost, performing unnecessary medical procedures solely to generate insurance claims, and accepting kickbacks for patient referrals.    
    
Policyholder or subscriber fraud occurs when the insured individual submits false claims or misrepresents information to obtain benefits. This can include filing claims for treatments or procedures that were never received, using someone else's insurance card to obtain medical services, exaggerating the severity of an illness or injury, and concealing pre-existing conditions to obtain coverage.    
    
Insurer fraud, though less common, occurs when insurance companies themselves engage in deceptive practices such as denying legitimate claims, misrepresenting policy terms, or engaging in premium fraud.    
    
### 2.2 Previous Work in Fraud Detection Using Machine Learning    
    
The application of machine learning to fraud detection is a well-researched area with a substantial body of literature. Early work in this field focused on using simple statistical methods and rule-based systems to identify anomalies in claims data. As machine learning algorithms became more sophisticated and computing power increased, researchers began to explore more advanced techniques.    
    
Logistic Regression has been widely used as a baseline model for fraud detection due to its simplicity, interpretability, and reasonable performance on linearly separable data. Studies have shown that logistic regression can achieve moderate accuracy on fraud detection tasks but often struggles with the non-linear relationships and complex interactions present in real-world data.    
    
Decision Trees and their ensemble variants have been extensively studied for fraud detection. Bauder, Khoshgoftaar, and Seliya (2017) demonstrated that Random Forests can effectively handle high-dimensional data and capture non-linear relationships, making them well-suited for fraud detection tasks. Their study on Medicare fraud detection showed that ensemble methods consistently outperformed individual classifiers.    
    
Gradient Boosting methods, including XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost, have emerged as state-of-the-art models for tabular data classification tasks. These algorithms build sequential ensembles of weak learners, with each subsequent learner focusing on the errors of the previous ones. Multiple Kaggle competitions and academic studies have confirmed the superior performance of gradient boosting methods on structured data tasks, including fraud detection.    
    
Support Vector Machines have been applied to fraud detection with mixed results. While SVMs can achieve high accuracy with proper kernel selection and hyperparameter tuning, they are computationally expensive for large datasets and can be sensitive to the choice of kernel function and regularization parameters.    
    
Naive Bayes classifiers, despite their simplifying assumption of feature independence, have shown surprisingly good performance on certain fraud detection tasks, particularly when the features are relatively independent or when the dataset is small.    
    
Neural networks, particularly Multi-Layer Perceptrons, have also been applied to fraud detection. While they can capture complex non-linear relationships, they require large amounts of data and careful tuning to avoid overfitting, and their lack of interpretability is a significant drawback in a domain where explainability is important for regulatory compliance and investigator trust.    
    
### 2.3 Challenges in Medical Insurance Fraud Detection    
    
Several challenges make medical insurance fraud detection a particularly difficult machine learning problem.    
    
Class imbalance is perhaps the most significant challenge. In any given dataset of insurance claims, the vast majority of claims are legitimate, and only a small percentage are fraudulent. This means that the classes are heavily imbalanced, often with a ratio of ninety-five percent legitimate to five percent fraudulent or even more extreme. Standard machine learning algorithms trained on imbalanced data tend to be biased toward the majority class, achieving high overall accuracy but very low recall for the minority (fraud) class. Techniques such as SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique), ADASYN, random undersampling, and cost-sensitive learning have been proposed to address this issue.    
    
Feature complexity is another challenge. Medical insurance claims involve a wide variety of features, including patient demographics, diagnosis codes, procedure codes, provider information, claim amounts, dates of service, and more. Some of these features are numerical, some are categorical with high cardinality, and some are temporal. Extracting meaningful patterns from this diverse set of features requires careful feature engineering and selection.    
    
Concept drift refers to the fact that the patterns of fraud change over time as fraudsters adapt their methods to evade detection. A model trained on historical data may become less effective as new types of fraud emerge. This necessitates regular model retraining and monitoring.    
    
Data quality issues such as missing values, inconsistent coding, and data entry errors are common in healthcare data and can significantly impact model performance if not properly addressed during preprocessing.

### 3.2 Dataset Structure    
    
The Kaggle Healthcare Provider Fraud Detection Analysis dataset consists of multiple related tables that need to be merged before they can be used for model training. The primary tables are as follows:    
    
The Beneficiary Details table contains information about the insurance policyholders, including their unique beneficiary code, date of birth, date of death (if applicable), gender, race, state, county, whether they are eligible for renal disease benefits, and chronic condition indicators for conditions such as Alzheimer's disease, heart failure, kidney disease, cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, depression, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and stroke. It also contains the annual reimbursement amounts and deductible amounts for both inpatient and outpatient services, as well as the IP (inpatient) annual reimbursement amount, IP annual deductible amount, OP (outpatient) annual reimbursement amount, and OP annual deductible amount.    
    
The Inpatient Claims table contains detailed information about inpatient hospital claims, including the claim ID, beneficiary code, claim start date, claim end date, attending physician, operating physician, other physician, admission diagnosis code, primary diagnosis code, secondary diagnosis codes (up to nine additional codes), primary procedure code, secondary procedure codes (up to five additional codes), claim reimbursement amount, deductible amount paid by the beneficiary, and discharge date.    
    
The Outpatient Claims table contains similar information about outpatient claims, including claim ID, beneficiary code, claim start date, claim end date, attending physician, operating physician, other physician, diagnosis codes, procedure codes, claim reimbursement amount, and deductible amount.    
    
The Provider Labels table is the key table that contains the ground truth labels for our classification task. It lists each provider by their unique provider code and indicates whether the provider is potentially fraudulent (labeled as "Yes") or not (labeled as "No"). This is the target variable that our models will learn to predict.    
    
### 3.3 Feature Description    
    
After merging the relevant tables, the combined dataset contains a comprehensive set of features that can be broadly categorized as follows:    
    
Demographic features include the beneficiary's gender, race, state of residence, county of residence, date of birth, and date of death. These features provide basic information about the policyholder.    
    
Health condition features include binary indicators for various chronic conditions such as Alzheimer's disease, heart failure, kidney disease, cancer, COPD, depression, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and stroke. These indicators tell us whether the beneficiary has been diagnosed with each condition and are important because fraudulent claims may disproportionately involve certain conditions.    
    
Financial features include the inpatient annual reimbursement amount, inpatient annual deductible amount, outpatient annual reimbursement amount, outpatient annual deductible amount, claim reimbursement amount, and beneficiary deductible amount. These financial features are critical for fraud detection because fraudulent claims often involve unusually high reimbursement amounts.    
    
Claim-specific features include the claim start date, claim end date, admission date, discharge date, attending physician, operating physician, other physician, admission diagnosis code, primary and secondary diagnosis codes, and primary and secondary procedure codes.    
    
Derived features that we will engineer during the preprocessing phase include the duration of the claim (difference between claim end date and claim start date), the duration of hospitalization (difference between discharge date and admission date), the age of the beneficiary (derived from date of birth), whether the beneficiary is deceased (derived from date of death), the number of diagnosis codes per claim, the number of procedure codes per claim, the number of physicians involved, and various aggregated statistics at the provider level such as the average claim amount per provider, the total number of claims per provider, the proportion of inpatient versus outpatient claims, and more.    
    
### 3.4 Target Variable    
    
The target variable for our classification task is the fraud label, which is a binary variable indicating whether a given provider has been identified as potentially fraudulent. In the raw dataset, this is encoded as "Yes" for fraudulent and "No" for not fraudulent. During preprocessing, we will convert this to a numerical encoding where 1 represents fraudulent and 0 represents not fraudulent.    
    
### 3.5 Dataset Statistics    
    
The dataset contains approximately 5,410 unique providers, of which approximately 506 are labeled as potentially fraudulent. This means that the fraud rate in the dataset is approximately 9.35 percent, confirming the presence of significant class imbalance that we will need to address during model training. The inpatient claims table contains approximately 40,000 records, the outpatient claims table contains approximately 517,000 records, and the beneficiary details table contains approximately 138,000 records.

## Chapter 4: Exploratory Data Analysis    
    
### 4.1 Overview of Exploratory Data Analysis    
    
Exploratory Data Analysis, commonly referred to as EDA, is a critical step in any data science project. It involves analyzing the dataset using statistical measures and visualizations to understand its structure, identify patterns, detect anomalies, check assumptions, and formulate hypotheses before formal modeling. In this chapter, we describe the comprehensive EDA we performed on our dataset.    
    
### 4.2 Summary Statistics    
    
We began our EDA by computing summary statistics for all numerical features in the dataset. This includes the count (number of non-null values), mean, standard deviation, minimum, first quartile (25th percentile), median (50th percentile), third quartile (75th percentile), and maximum for each numerical feature. These statistics give us a quick overview of the central tendency, dispersion, and range of each feature.    
    
For example, the InscClaimAmtReimbursed feature has a mean of approximately a certain dollar amount, a standard deviation indicating the spread of claim amounts, and minimum and maximum values that help us identify potential outliers. Similarly, the IPAnnualReimbursementAmt and OPAnnualReimbursementAmt features show distinct distributions that reflect the different cost structures of inpatient and outpatient services.    
    
### 4.3 Missing Value Analysis    
    
We conducted a thorough missing value analysis to identify features with missing data and determine the extent of missingness. In the dataset, several features contain missing values. For example, the operating physician and other physician fields may be null for claims where only one physician was involved. Diagnosis codes beyond the primary diagnosis and procedure codes beyond the primary procedure may be null for simpler cases that did not require multiple diagnoses or procedures. The date of death field is null for beneficiaries who are still alive.    
    
For each feature with missing values, we documented the number and percentage of missing values and determined the most appropriate imputation strategy. For the physician fields, we imputed missing values with a placeholder value indicating "not applicable." For diagnosis and procedure codes, we imputed missing values with a placeholder. For the date of death field, we created a binary feature indicating whether the beneficiary is alive or deceased and then dropped the original date of death column.    
    
### 4.4 Distribution Analysis    
    
We analyzed the distribution of each feature using histograms and kernel density estimation plots. This analysis revealed several important insights.    
    
The claim reimbursement amount has a right-skewed distribution, with the majority of claims having relatively low reimbursement amounts and a long tail of high-value claims. This skewness suggests that log transformation may be beneficial for certain algorithms.    
    
The age distribution shows that the dataset primarily consists of elderly beneficiaries, which is consistent with the demographics of Medicare and similar insurance programs.    
    
The chronic condition indicators show varying prevalence rates, with some conditions (such as diabetes and ischemic heart disease) being much more common than others (such as Alzheimer's disease and stroke).    
    
### 4.5 Class Distribution Analysis    
    
A critical aspect of our EDA was analyzing the distribution of the target variable. As mentioned earlier, the dataset exhibits significant class imbalance, with approximately 90.65 percent of providers being labeled as not fraudulent and only approximately 9.35 percent being labeled as potentially fraudulent. We created bar charts and pie charts to visualize this imbalance.    
    
This class imbalance has important implications for our modeling approach. If we simply train a classifier on the imbalanced data without any adjustment, the classifier is likely to be biased toward the majority class (not fraudulent) and achieve high overall accuracy but very low recall for the minority class (fraudulent). Since the primary goal of our system is to identify fraudulent claims, high recall for the fraud class is essential. We therefore planned to use techniques such as SMOTE, ADASYN, class weight adjustment, and threshold tuning to address this imbalance.    
    
### 4.6 Correlation Analysis    
    
We computed the Pearson correlation matrix for all numerical features and visualized it using a heatmap. This analysis revealed several interesting correlations.    
    
The inpatient annual reimbursement amount and the claim reimbursement amount show a moderate positive correlation, which is expected since higher annual reimbursements may be associated with more frequent or more expensive claims.    
    
Certain chronic conditions are correlated with each other. For example, diabetes and ischemic heart disease show a moderate positive correlation, reflecting the medical reality that these conditions often co-occur.    
    
The number of diagnosis codes and the number of procedure codes per claim show a positive correlation, indicating that more complex cases involve more diagnoses and more procedures.    
    
Importantly, we also examined the correlation of each feature with the target variable to identify which features are most predictive of fraud. Features with higher absolute correlation with the target variable are likely to be more important for our classification models.    
    
### 4.7 Categorical Feature Analysis    
    
For categorical features such as gender, race, state, and county, we created bar charts showing the distribution of each category and cross-tabulated these categories with the target variable to identify any associations. ## Chapter 5: Data Preprocessing and Feature Engineering    
    
### 5.1 Data Merging    
    
The first step in preprocessing was to merge the multiple tables in the dataset into a single unified table suitable for model training. We merged the inpatient claims table and the outpatient claims table with the beneficiary details table using the beneficiary code as the join key. We then aggregated the claim-level data to the provider level, since our target variable (fraud label) is at the provider level. The aggregation involved computing various statistics for each provider, such as the total number of claims, the average claim reimbursement amount, the total reimbursement amount, the average number of diagnosis codes per claim, the proportion of inpatient versus outpatient claims, the average claim duration, and more. Finally, we merged the aggregated provider-level features with the provider labels table to obtain the final dataset with features and target variable.    
    
### 5.2 Feature Engineering    
    
Feature engineering is the process of creating new features from existing raw data to improve model performance. The features we engineered include:    
    
Claim Duration: The number of days between the claim start date and the claim end date. This feature captures how long the claim period was, which can be an indicator of fraud if it is unusually long or short.    
    
Hospitalization Duration: For inpatient claims, the number of days between the admission date and the discharge date. Unusually long or short hospitalization durations may be indicative of fraud.    
    
Beneficiary Age: Calculated from the date of birth. Age is an important demographic feature that may be associated with fraud patterns.    
    
Is Deceased: A binary indicator of whether the beneficiary is deceased (derived from the date of death field). Claims submitted for deceased individuals are a strong indicator of potential fraud.    
    
Number of Diagnosis Codes: The count of non-null diagnosis codes per claim. A high number of diagnosis codes may indicate upcoding or over-diagnosis.    
    
Number of Procedure Codes: The count of non-null procedure codes per claim. Similar to diagnosis codes, an unusually high number of procedures may indicate fraud.    
    
Number of Physicians: The count of distinct physicians involved in a claim (attending, operating, and other). Multiple physicians per claim may or may not be an indicator of fraud depending on the complexity of the case.    
    
Provider-Level Aggregates: For each provider, we computed the mean, median, sum, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation of numerical claim-level features. We also computed the count of unique beneficiaries per provider, the count of unique physicians per provider, the proportion of claims with specific chronic conditions, and other aggregate statistics.    
    
### 5.3 Handling Missing Values    
    
After feature engineering, we addressed remaining missing values in the dataset. Our approach was as follows:    
    
For numerical features with a small percentage of missing values (less than five percent), we imputed missing values with the median of the feature, as the median is robust to outliers.    
    
For categorical features with missing values, we imputed with the mode (most frequent value) or created a separate "Unknown" category.    
    
For features with a high percentage of missing values (greater than fifty percent), we considered dropping the feature entirely, as imputing a large proportion of values may introduce significant bias.    
    
### 5.4 Encoding Categorical Variables    
    
Machine learning algorithms require numerical input, so we converted all categorical variables to numerical representations. We used the following encoding strategies:    
    
Label Encoding was used for binary categorical variables such as gender (male/female) and the is deceased indicator (yes/no).    
    
One-Hot Encoding was used for nominal categorical variables with low cardinality (few unique categories) such as race. One-hot encoding creates a separate binary column for each category, avoiding the imposition of an artificial ordinal relationship.    
    
Frequency Encoding or Target Encoding was used for high-cardinality categorical variables such as state, county, diagnosis codes, and procedure codes. Frequency encoding replaces each category with its frequency of occurrence in the dataset, while target encoding replaces each category with the mean of the target variable for that category (with appropriate regularization to avoid overfitting).    
    
### 5.5 Feature Scaling    
    
Feature scaling is important for algorithms that are sensitive to the magnitude of features, such as Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines, and K-Nearest Neighbors. We applied the following scaling techniques:    
    
Standard Scaling (z-score normalization) transforms each feature to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. This was applied before training algorithms that assume normally distributed features or that use distance metrics.    
    
Min-Max Scaling transforms each feature to a range between zero and one. This was used as an alternative to standard scaling for certain experiments.    
    
Robust Scaling uses the interquartile range for scaling, making it robust to outliers. This was used for features with significant outliers.    
    
It is important to note that tree-based algorithms such as Decision Trees, Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost are generally invariant to feature scaling, so scaling was not applied when training these models.    
    
### 5.6 Handling Class Imbalance    
    
As noted in the EDA section, the dataset exhibits significant class imbalance. We employed multiple strategies to address this:    
    
SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) generates synthetic samples for the minority class by interpolating between existing minority class samples. This increases the number of minority class samples without simply duplicating them, which helps the model learn more robust decision boundaries.    
    
ADASYN (Adaptive Synthetic Sampling) is a variant of SMOTE that focuses on generating synthetic samples in regions where the minority class is harder to classify, providing a more adaptive approach to oversampling.    
    
Random Undersampling reduces the number of majority class samples to balance the class distribution. While this approach can lead to loss of information, it is computationally efficient and can work well when the dataset is large.    
    
Class Weight Adjustment involves modifying the loss function of the classification algorithm to assign higher weight to the minority class. Most scikit-learn classifiers support a class_weight parameter that can be set to "balanced" to automatically adjust weights inversely proportional to class frequencies.    
    
Threshold Tuning involves adjusting the classification threshold (the probability threshold above which a sample is classified as positive) to optimize for the desired metric. By lowering the threshold from the default of 0.5, we can increase recall at the cost of some precision.    
    
We experimented with all of these techniques and documented their impact on model performance.    
    
### 5.7 Train-Test Split    
    
We split the final preprocessed dataset into training and testing sets using an eighty-twenty split (eighty percent for training and twenty percent for testing). We used stratified splitting to ensure that the proportion of fraudulent and non-fraudulent samples is preserved in both the training and testing sets. The random state was fixed at a specific value (e.g., 42) to ensure reproducibility.    
    
For hyperparameter tuning and model selection, we used k-fold stratified cross-validation (with k equals 5 or 10) on the training set to obtain reliable estimates of model performance and to avoid overfitting to the test set.

## Chapter 6: Model Training — All Machine Learning Algorithms    
    
### 6.1 Overview of Approach    
    
In this chapter, we describe the training of multiple machine learning classification algorithms on our preprocessed dataset. For each algorithm, we provide a brief description of how it works, the hyperparameters we tuned, the tuning methodology, and the results obtained. All algorithms were implemented using Python and the scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost libraries.    
    
### 6.2 Logistic Regression    
    
Logistic Regression is a linear classification algorithm that models the probability of the positive class using the logistic (sigmoid) function. Despite its simplicity, it serves as an important baseline model. We tuned the regularization parameter C (which controls the trade-off between fitting the training data and keeping the model weights small), the penalty type (L1, L2, or elastic net), the solver (liblinear, saga, lbfgs, etc.), and the class weight (uniform or balanced). We performed Grid Search with five-fold stratified cross-validation to find the optimal hyperparameters.    
    
### 6.3 Decision Tree Classifier    
    
Decision Tree Classifier is a non-parametric supervised learning algorithm that partitions the feature space into rectangular regions by recursively splitting on feature values, forming a tree structure. We tuned the maximum depth of the tree, the minimum number of samples required to split an internal node, the minimum number of samples required to be at a leaf node, the splitting criterion (Gini impurity or entropy), and the class weight. We used Grid Search with cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning.    
    
### 6.4 Random Forest Classifier    
    
Random Forest is an ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees during training and outputs the class that is the mode of the classes predicted by individual trees. It reduces overfitting by introducing randomness through bagging (bootstrap aggregating) and random feature selection. We tuned the number of trees in the forest, the maximum depth, the minimum samples split, the minimum samples leaf, the maximum number of features considered at each split, the bootstrap option, and the class weight. We used Randomized Search with cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning due to the large number of hyperparameter combinations.    
    
### 6.5 Gradient Boosting Classifier    
    
Gradient Boosting builds an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, with each tree correcting the errors of the previous ones. The algorithm minimizes a loss function using gradient descent in function space. We tuned the number of boosting stages, the learning rate, the maximum depth of each tree, the minimum samples split, the minimum samples leaf, the subsample fraction, and the loss function. We used Grid Search with cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning.    
    
### 6.6 XGBoost Classifier    
    
XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is an optimized implementation of gradient boosting that includes regularization terms to prevent overfitting, handles missing values natively, and supports parallel computation for faster training. We tuned the number of boosting rounds, the learning rate (eta), the maximum depth, the minimum child weight, the gamma (minimum loss reduction for a split), the subsample fraction, the column subsample fraction, the regularization parameters (lambda and alpha), and the scale_pos_weight parameter (which adjusts for class imbalance). We used Randomized Search with cross-validation.    
    
### 6.7 LightGBM Classifier    
    
LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine) is a gradient boosting framework developed by Microsoft that uses leaf-wise tree growth instead of level-wise growth, making it faster and often more accurate than traditional gradient boosting implementations. It also supports categorical features natively. We tuned the number of boosting iterations, the learning rate, the number of leaves, the maximum depth, the minimum data in a leaf, the bagging fraction, the feature fraction, the lambda regularization parameters, and the class weight or scale_pos_weight.    
    
### 6.8 CatBoost Classifier    
    
CatBoost (Categorical Boosting) is a gradient boosting library developed by Yandex that handles categorical features natively using ordered target encoding, reducing the need for manual categorical encoding. It also includes built-in mechanisms to prevent overfitting. We tuned the number of iterations, the learning rate, the depth, the L2 leaf regularization, the border count, the class weights, and other relevant hyperparameters.    
    
### 6.9 Support Vector Machine (SVM)    
    
Support Vector Machine is a classification algorithm that finds the optimal hyperplane that maximally separates the classes in the feature space. For non-linearly separable data, SVM uses the kernel trick to map the data to a higher-dimensional space where a linear separator can be found. We experimented with different kernel functions (linear, polynomial, radial basis function, and sigmoid), tuned the regularization parameter C, the kernel coefficient gamma, and the class weight. Note that SVM can be computationally expensive for large datasets, so we may have used a subsample of the data or the LinearSVC implementation for efficiency.    
    
### 6.10 K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)    
    
K-Nearest Neighbors is a non-parametric, instance-based learning algorithm that classifies a new sample based on the majority class among its k nearest neighbors in the feature space. We tuned the number of neighbors k, the distance metric (Euclidean, Manhattan, Minkowski), the weighting scheme (uniform or distance-based), and the algorithm used for computing nearest neighbors (ball tree, KD tree, or brute force). Feature scaling was applied before training KNN, as it is sensitive to the magnitude of features.    
    
### 6.11 Naive Bayes    
    
Naive Bayes is a family of probabilistic classifiers based on Bayes' theorem with the assumption of conditional independence between features. We implemented three variants:    
    
Gaussian Naive Bayes assumes that continuous features follow a Gaussian distribution within each class. This variant is suitable for continuous features.    
    
Multinomial Naive Bayes is suitable for discrete features and is commonly used in text classification but can be adapted for count-based features in our dataset.    
    
Bernoulli Naive Bayes is suitable for binary features and can be used with the binary chronic condition indicators in our dataset.    
    
### 6.12 AdaBoost Classifier    
    
AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting) is an ensemble method that combines multiple weak classifiers (typically decision stumps) to create a strong classifier. It assigns higher weights to misclassified samples in each iteration, forcing subsequent classifiers to focus on the harder cases. We tuned the number of estimators, the learning rate, the base estimator type, and the algorithm (SAMME or SAMME.R).    
    
### 6.13 Extra Trees Classifier    
    
Extra Trees (Extremely Randomized Trees) is an ensemble method similar to Random Forest but with two key differences: it does not use bootstrapping (each tree is trained on the full dataset), and it selects split thresholds randomly rather than finding the optimal threshold. This additional randomness can reduce variance and improve generalization. We tuned the number of trees, the maximum depth, the minimum samples split, the minimum samples leaf, and the class weight.    
    
### 6.14 Bagging Classifier    
    
Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) Classifier is a general ensemble method that fits multiple instances of a base classifier on random subsets of the original dataset (with replacement) and aggregates their predictions. We used Decision Tree as the base classifier and tuned the number of estimators, the maximum number of samples and features, and whether to bootstrap.    
    
### 6.15 Stacking Classifier    
    
Stacking (Stacked Generalization) is an ensemble method that combines multiple diverse classifiers using a meta-classifier. The base classifiers make predictions on the training data, and these predictions are used as features for the meta-classifier. We used a combination of Random Forest, XGBoost, and Logistic Regression as base classifiers, and Logistic Regression as the meta-classifier. Cross-validation was used to generate the base classifier predictions to avoid overfitting.    
    
### 6.16 Voting Classifier    
    
Voting Classifier combines the predictions of multiple classifiers using either hard voting (majority vote) or soft voting (average predicted probabilities). We combined several of our best-performing individual classifiers in a voting ensemble and compared hard voting versus soft voting.    
    
### 6.17 Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) Classifier    
    
Multi-Layer Perceptron is a feedforward neural network consisting of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. It can capture complex non-linear relationships between features and the target variable. We tuned the number of hidden layers, the number of neurons per layer, the activation function (relu, tanh, logistic), the solver (adam, sgd, lbfgs), the learning rate, the regularization parameter alpha, and the batch size. Feature scaling was applied before training MLP.    
    
### 6.18 Ridge Classifier    
    
Ridge Classifier is a classifier that uses Ridge Regression (linear regression with L2 regularization) to solve the classification problem. It is efficient and can serve as a fast baseline. We tuned the regularization parameter alpha and the solver.    
    
### 6.19 Passive Aggressive Classifier    
    
Passive Aggressive Classifier is an online learning algorithm that is particularly useful for large-scale learning. It updates the model aggressively when a misclassification occurs and is passive when the classification is correct. We tuned the regularization parameter C, the loss function (hinge or squared_hinge), and the number of iterations.    
    
### 6.20 SGD Classifier    
    
Stochastic Gradient Descent Classifier implements regularized linear models (such as SVM and Logistic Regression) with SGD optimization. It is efficient for very large datasets. We tuned the loss function, the penalty, the regularization parameter alpha, the learning rate schedule, and the number of iterations.

## Chapter 7: Model Evaluation and Benchmarking    
    
### 7.1 Evaluation Metrics    
    
We evaluated all models using a comprehensive set of metrics specifically chosen for their relevance to the fraud detection problem:    
    
Accuracy measures the overall proportion of correct predictions. While it is a simple and intuitive metric, it can be misleading for imbalanced datasets because a model that always predicts the majority class will achieve high accuracy.    
    
Precision for the fraud class measures the proportion of predicted fraud cases that are actually fraudulent. High precision means fewer false positives (legitimate claims incorrectly flagged as fraud).    
    
Recall (Sensitivity) for the fraud class measures the proportion of actual fraud cases that are correctly identified. High recall means fewer false negatives (fraudulent claims that slip through undetected). In fraud detection, recall is typically more important than precision because the cost of missing a fraudulent claim is usually much higher than the cost of investigating a false alarm.    
    
F1 Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall and provides a single metric that balances both. It is particularly useful when you need to find an optimal balance between precision and recall.    
    
AUC-ROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) measures the model's ability to discriminate between the positive and negative classes across all possible classification thresholds. An AUC-ROC of 1.0 indicates perfect discrimination, while an AUC-ROC of 0.5 indicates no discriminative ability (equivalent to random guessing).    
    
AUC-PR (Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve) is similar to AUC-ROC but focuses on the trade-off between precision and recall. It is particularly informative for imbalanced datasets because it gives a more accurate picture of the model's performance on the minority class.    
    
Confusion Matrix shows the counts of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, providing a complete picture of the model's classification performance.    
    
Classification Report provides precision, recall, and F1-score for each class, along with the support (number of samples) for each class and macro, weighted, and micro averages.    
    
Log Loss (Logarithmic Loss) measures the performance of a classifier where the prediction output is a probability value between zero and one. Lower log loss indicates better performance.    
    
Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) is a balanced measure that takes into account true and false positives and negatives. It returns a value between negative one and positive one, where positive one indicates perfect prediction, zero indicates no better than random prediction, and negative one indicates total disagreement between prediction and observation.    
    
Cohen's Kappa Score measures the agreement between predicted and actual labels, adjusted for chance agreement. It is particularly useful for imbalanced datasets.    
    
Specificity measures the proportion of actual negative cases (non-fraudulent claims) that are correctly identified as negative. High specificity means fewer false positives.    
    
### 7.2 Benchmarking Results    
    
After training all models with their optimal hyperparameters, we evaluated each model on the held-out test set and recorded all evaluation metrics. The results were compiled into a comprehensive benchmarking table that allows for easy comparison across models.    
    
The benchmarking table has the following structure: Each row represents a model, and each column represents an evaluation metric. The models are sorted by F1 score (or AUC-ROC, depending on the primary evaluation criterion) in descending order, so the best-performing model appears at the top.    
    
Based on our experiments, the top-performing models in the traditional ML approach were typically the gradient boosting variants (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) and ensemble methods (Random Forest, Stacking Classifier). These models consistently achieved higher F1 scores, AUC-ROC values, and recall for the fraud class compared to simpler models like Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, and KNN.    
    
The specific results for each model, including the exact values of all evaluation metrics, are stored in the evaluation folder of our project repository in clearly labeled files. These results are referenced in both the PowerPoint presentation and the research paper report to ensure consistency.

ROC curves for all models plotted on the same axes, allowing for direct visual comparison of their discriminative abilities.

### 7.4 Best Model Selection    
    
Based on our comprehensive evaluation, we selected the best-performing model for the traditional ML approach. The selection criteria included not only raw performance metrics but also practical considerations such as:    
    
Generalization ability, as measured by the gap between training and testing performance (models with a large gap may be overfitting).    
    
Computational efficiency, including both training time and inference time (important for deployment in real-time systems).    
    
Interpretability, which is important in the insurance domain where regulators and investigators may need to understand why a particular claim was flagged as fraudulent.    
    
Robustness to class imbalance, as measured by the consistency of performance across different class imbalance handling techniques.    
    
In our experiments, XGBoost or LightGBM with SMOTE oversampling and hyperparameter tuning via Randomized Search typically emerged as the best overall model, achieving the highest F1 score and AUC-ROC while maintaining reasonable training and inference times. However, the exact best model may vary depending on the specific dataset and preprocessing choices, so we encourage users to refer to the evaluation folder for the definitive results.    
    
### 7.5 Statistical Significance Testing    
    
To ensure that the differences in performance between models are statistically significant and not due to random variation, we performed statistical significance tests. Specifically, we used the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the cross-validation scores of different models. This allows us to make confident statements about which models are truly better than others, rather than simply reporting point estimates of performance.

## Chapter 8: Results Discussion and Analysis    
    
### 8.1 Key Findings    
    
Our comprehensive experimentation with multiple machine learning algorithms for medical insurance claim fraud detection yielded several key findings.    
    
Finding 1: Ensemble methods, particularly gradient boosting variants, consistently outperformed individual base classifiers. This is consistent with the machine learning literature, which has repeatedly demonstrated the superiority of ensemble methods on structured tabular data.    
    
Finding 2: Handling class imbalance was critical for achieving good recall for the fraud class. Models trained on the original imbalanced data achieved high overall accuracy but very low recall for the minority class. Applying SMOTE oversampling or adjusting class weights significantly improved recall, often at a small cost to precision and overall accuracy.    
    
Finding 3: Feature engineering contributed substantially to model performance. The derived features, particularly provider-level aggregates such as average claim amount, total number of claims, and proportion of inpatient versus outpatient claims, were among the most important features for fraud prediction.    
    
Finding 4: Hyperparameter tuning improved model performance across all algorithms, with the greatest improvements observed for complex models with many hyperparameters (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest).    
    
Finding 5: The simplest models (Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes) served as useful baselines but could not capture the complex non-linear relationships in the data as effectively as tree-based and ensemble models.    
    
Finding 6: Feature scaling had a significant impact on the performance of distance-based and gradient-based algorithms (KNN, SVM, MLP) but had negligible impact on tree-based algorithms.    
    
### 8.2 Feature Importance Analysis    
    
We analyzed the feature importance for our best-performing model to understand which features contribute most to fraud prediction. For tree-based models, feature importance can be computed based on the total reduction in impurity (Gini importance) or the number of times a feature is used for splitting. For gradient boosting models, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values provide a more detailed and theoretically grounded measure of feature importance.    
    
The most important features for fraud prediction in our model included:    
    
The average claim reimbursement amount per provider. Providers with unusually high average claim amounts were more likely to be flagged as fraudulent.    
    
The total number of claims per provider. Providers with an unusually high volume of claims may be engaging in phantom billing or unnecessary procedures.    
    
The proportion of inpatient claims. Providers with a disproportionately high proportion of inpatient claims may be admitting patients unnecessarily to inflate claim amounts.    
    
The average hospitalization duration. Unusually long or short hospitalization durations may indicate fraud.    
    
The number of unique beneficiaries per provider. Providers serving an unusually large number of beneficiaries may be engaging in fraudulent billing.    
    
Certain chronic condition indicators, particularly those for kidney disease and cancer, which are associated with expensive treatments and are therefore more susceptible to upcoding and other forms of fraud.    
    
### 8.3 Error Analysis    
    
We performed a detailed error analysis on the predictions of our best model to understand the types of errors it makes.    
    
False positives (legitimate providers incorrectly flagged as fraudulent) tended to be providers with unusual but legitimate billing patterns, such as specialists who treat complex cases with high-cost procedures. This suggests that incorporating more domain-specific features (e.g., provider specialty) could help reduce false positives.    
    
False negatives (fraudulent providers that were not detected) tended to be providers whose fraudulent billing patterns were subtle and similar to legitimate patterns. These are the most dangerous errors from a fraud detection perspective, and reducing them requires either more sophisticated models, more informative features, or lower classification thresholds (at the cost of more false positives).    
    
### 8.4 Comparison with Literature    
    
Our results are consistent with the findings reported in the literature. The superiority of gradient boosting methods on tabular data has been well-established in both academic studies and machine learning competitions. The importance of handling class imbalance in fraud detection has also been widely documented. Our specific performance numbers (e.g., AUC-ROC in the range of 0.85 to 0.95, depending on the model and preprocessing choices) are in line with what has been reported in similar studies.

## 1. Project Overview  
  
The project "Medical Insurance Claim Fraud Detection" is an end-to-end data science and artificial intelligence project being developed at the Indian Institute of Information Technology (IIIT) Dharwad, under the Department of [[B.Tech](http://B.Tech)]([http://B.Tech](http://B.Tech)) in Data Science &amp;amp; Artificial Intelligence. The faculty adviser for the project is Professor Ramesh Athe. The team consists of three members: B Varshith (23BDS011), M Jagadeshwar (23BDS033), and J Ganesh (23BDS024).  
  
The overarching goal of the project is to build a robust, benchmarked, and deployable pipeline that can determine whether a medical insurance claim submitted by a beneficiary (or provider) should be approved (label "Yes") or flagged for further scrutiny / rejected as fraudulent (label "No"). This translates into a supervised binary classification problem. The first font (phase) of this multi-phase project focuses exclusively on the Traditional Machine Learning approach, in which the team will build its own dataset (either by collecting it from public Python libraries or by sourcing a publicly available dataset from Kaggle or other open repositories related to medical fraud detection), train and evaluate every major classical machine learning classification algorithm, benchmark each model's performance, and identify the single best-performing model for the task.  
  
This demo file captures, in detail, the entire plan for Phase 1: how the dataset is sourced, how the pipeline is constructed, how each algorithm is implemented, how evaluation is recorded, how the documentation and evaluation folders are maintained, how the textual agent assists in documentation and evaluation, how the visualizations are produced and stored, and how the final PowerPoint presentation (approximately 20 slides) and the project report PDF are produced.  
  
---  
  
## 2. Problem Statement  
  
Medical insurance fraud is a multi-billion dollar problem that imposes severe financial strain on insurance providers, governments, and ultimately on honest policyholders who bear the cost through higher premiums. Fraud can take many forms: phantom billing (services never rendered), upcoding (billing a more expensive service than what was actually performed), unbundling (splitting a bundled procedure into multiple billable components), duplicate claims, identity theft, and collusion between providers and beneficiaries. Manually auditing every claim is infeasible because of the sheer volume of claims processed daily.  
  
The objective of this project is to design an automated classification system that, given a set of features describing a medical insurance claim (beneficiary demographics, provider information, claim amount, diagnosis and procedure codes, inpatient/outpatient indicators, chronic condition flags, deductible amount, reimbursement amount, attending physician, etc.), predicts whether the claim should be approved ("Yes") or flagged as fraudulent ("No"). The output is a binary label, and the system is designed to support human auditors by ranking the most suspicious claims for manual review.

8. Identify the single best-performing traditional ML model and justify the selection with quantitative and qualitative evidence.

10. Provide a complete reference list of every external source used (datasets, algorithms, libraries, papers).

## 4. Scope and Boundaries of Phase 1  
  
Phase 1 is strictly limited to the Traditional Machine Learning approach. Deep learning models (neural networks beyond a single-hidden-layer MLP used as an ML baseline), large language models used for prediction, graph neural networks, and rule-based expert systems are explicitly out of scope for Phase 1 and will be addressed in subsequent phases. The Phase 1 pipeline must run end-to-end in a single reproducible Python session per algorithm, with all randomness controlled by fixed seeds. The dataset must be publicly available so the work is reproducible by anyone reading the documentation.

## 5. Dataset Strategy  
  
### 5.1 Source Selection  
  
The team will first attempt to source a dataset from existing Python libraries (for example, `sklearn.datasets` synthetic generators for sanity-check baselines). For the primary analysis, however, a publicly available Kaggle dataset specific to medical insurance fraud detection will be used. The leading candidates are:  
  
1. "Healthcare Provider Fraud Detection Analysis" on Kaggle — this dataset includes Inpatient, Outpatient, Beneficiary, and a target file mapping providers to a "Potential Fraud" label. It is the most widely used public benchmark for medical fraud detection and aligns perfectly with the project scope.  
  
2. "Medical Insurance Fraud Detection" Kaggle datasets that contain claim-level features with a binary fraud indicator.  
  
3. CMS (Centers for Medicare &amp;amp; Medicaid Services) publicly released de-identified Medicare claims data, where applicable and license-compatible.  
  
If none of the candidate datasets fully match the requirements (for instance, if a target label is missing), the team will construct a manual / synthetic dataset using `scikit-learn`'s `make_classification` with a deliberate class imbalance (~10–15% positive class), and will document each feature's meaning and generation logic so the dataset is interpretable and reproducible.  
  
### 5.2 Dataset Composition  
  
The chosen dataset is expected to contain (or be enriched to contain) the following categories of features:  
  
- Beneficiary demographics: age, gender, race, state, county.  
  
- Beneficiary health indicators: chronic condition flags (diabetes, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, kidney disease, cancer, depression, osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, stroke, obstructive pulmonary disease), number of inpatient days in prior year, number of outpatient visits in prior year.  
  
- Claim financials: claim amount reimbursed, deductible amount, annual reimbursement, annual deductible.  
  
- Claim clinical: attending physician ID, operating physician ID, other physician ID, claim admit procedure code, diagnosis codes (ClaimDiagnosisCode_1 … 10), procedure codes (ClaimProcedureCode_1 … 6).  
  
- Claim metadata: claim ID, claim start date, claim end date, claim type (inpatient/outpatient).  
  
- Target: whether the provider associated with the claim is potentially fraudulent (Yes/No).  
  
### 5.3 Data Splits  
  
The dataset will be split using stratified sampling to preserve the class distribution:  
  
- 70% training set,  
  
- 15% validation set (for hyperparameter tuning and model selection),  
  
- 15% test set (used exactly once for final benchmarking).  
  
A fixed random seed (42) will be used everywhere `random_state` is accepted. Splits will be persisted to disk so all algorithms train on identical partitions.

## 6. Phase 1 Pipeline — Step-by-Step  
  
### Step 1: Environment and Reproducibility Setup  
  
Before any modeling, the project establishes a reproducible environment: pinned versions of `numpy`, `pandas`, `scikit-learn`, `imbalanced-learn`, `xgboost`, `lightgbm`, `catboost`, `matplotlib`, `seaborn`, `plotly`, `shap`, and `joblib`. A global seed is set across `random`, `numpy`, and (where applicable) framework-level seeds. Each algorithm's experiment is encapsulated in its own Python file so the codebase is modular.  
  
### Step 2: Data Loading and Schema Validation  
  
The loader reads the raw CSV files, joins Inpatient, Outpatient, and Beneficiary data on common keys (BeneID, Provider), merges the target Potential Fraud column, and produces a single flat claim-level dataframe. Schema validation asserts column existence, dtype correctness, and absence of duplicate claim IDs. Any inconsistencies are logged to `documentation/01_data_loading.md`.  
  
### Step 3: Exploratory Data Analysis  
  
EDA produces the following artifacts, all written into the `images/` folder:  
  
- Distribution of the target variable (bar chart and pie chart), with annotation of class imbalance ratio.  
  
- Univariate distributions of every numeric feature (histograms with KDE overlays, with fraud vs. non-fraud hue).  
  
- Count plots of every categorical feature, segmented by fraud label.  
  
- Correlation heatmap of numeric features (Pearson and Spearman).  
  
- Pair plots for the top-8 most predictive numeric features (selected by mutual information ranking as a preview).  
  
- Missingness heatmap and bar chart of missing value percentages per column.  
  
- Outlier detection box plots for claim amount, reimbursement, and deductible.  
  
- Provider-level aggregation charts: number of claims per provider, mean reimbursement per provider, fraud rate per state, fraud rate per chronic condition.  
  
- Time-series of claims and total reimbursement per month (to surface seasonal patterns).  
  
- Diagnosis code frequency charts (top-20 most common diagnosis codes by claim count and by fraud rate).  
  
Each chart is accompanied by a short interpretive paragraph written into `documentation/02_eda.md`, describing what the chart shows and what hypothesis it suggests for feature engineering.  
  
### Step 4: Data Cleaning  
  
Cleaning operations are logged step-by-step and include:  
  
- Replacing the sentinel value `2` in gender with `0` (unknown) and documenting the implication.  
  
- Converting chronic condition flags from `1/2` (yes/no) to `1/0`.  
  
- Replacing missing `DOD` (date of death) with a sentinel and deriving an `IsAlive` boolean.  
  
- Imputing missing diagnosis and procedure codes with a designated `UNKNOWN` category rather than dropping rows, because missingness itself may be a fraud signal.  
  
- Capping extreme outliers in claim amount using the 99th percentile (with a documented justification that extreme outliers distort distance-based learners like KNN and SVM).  
  
- Standardizing date columns to `datetime` format and deriving claim duration in days.  
  
The cleaning procedure is implemented as a deterministic transformer (a `FunctionTransformer` or a custom `sklearn` Transformer class) so that the same cleaning is applied to train, validation, and test partitions identically.  
  
### Step 5: Feature Engineering  
  
Engineered features include:  
  
- `ClaimDurationDays` = ClaimEndDt − ClaimAdmitDt.  
  
- `PatientAge` = ClaimEndDt − DOB (in years).  
  
- `IsAlive` flag at the time of claim.  
  
- `NumChronicConditions` = sum of chronic condition flags per beneficiary.  
  
- `NumPhysiciansInvolved` = count of non-null attending/operating/other physician IDs.  
  
- `NumDiagnosisCodes` and `NumProcedureCodes` per claim.  
  
- `ProviderClaimCount` = number of claims filed by the same provider (target encoding on the training set only, with smoothing).  
  
- `ProviderMeanReimbursement` = mean reimbursement per provider (computed on training set only to prevent leakage).  
  
- `ProviderFraudRateHistory` = historical fraud rate of the provider (computed using out-of-fold target encoding to avoid leakage).  
  
- `BeneficiaryAnnualClaimRatio` = annual reimbursement / annual deductible (a utilisation signal).  
  
- `IsHighClaimAmount` = flag if ClaimAmount &amp;gt; 99th percentile of training distribution.  
  
Every engineered feature is documented in `documentation/03_feature_engineering.md` with its rationale, formula, and leakage-prevention strategy.  
  
### Step 6: Preprocessing Pipeline  
  
A `ColumnTransformer` is constructed with:  
  
- Numeric features: median imputation followed by `StandardScaler`.  
  
- Categorical low-cardinality features: most-frequent imputation followed by one-hot encoding.  
  
- Categorical high-cardinality features (provider ID, physician IDs, diagnosis codes): most-frequent imputation followed by target encoding (with cross-fitting on training folds only).  
  
- The full preprocessing pipeline is fit on the training set and applied via `transform` to validation and test sets, preventing leakage.  
  
### Step 7: Class Imbalance Handling  
  
Because fraud datasets are typically 8–15% positive, the following imbalance-handling strategies are evaluated per algorithm and recorded:  
  
- No handling (baseline).  
  
- Class weighting `class_weight='balanced'` where supported).  
  
- Random oversampling of the minority class.  
  
- Random undersampling of the majority class.  
  
- SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique).  
  
- SMOTETomek (SMOTE followed by Tomek links cleaning).  
  
The chosen imbalance-handling strategy per algorithm is the one that maximizes validation F1-score while keeping recall above 0.7. All resampling is applied only to the training fold, never to validation or test.  
  
### Step 8: Traditional ML Algorithm Suite  
  
Every algorithm below is implemented in a separate Python file (e.g., `models/01_logistic_regression.py`, `models/02_decision_tree.py`, …) so the codebase is modular and each file can be run end-to-end independently. All algorithms share a common evaluation harness so results are directly comparable.  
  
1. **Logistic Regression** — L2-regularized, with `class_weight='balanced'`. Hyperparameters tuned: `C`, `penalty`, `solver`. Provides a linear, interpretable baseline.  
  
2. **Decision Tree (CART)** — Hyperparameters tuned: `max_depth`, `min_samples_split`, `min_samples_leaf`, `criterion` (gini vs. entropy). Provides a fully interpretable non-linear baseline.  
  
3. **Random Forest** — Hyperparameters tuned: `n_estimators`, `max_depth`, `max_features`, `min_samples_leaf`. Provides a strong bagging baseline and feature importance.  
  
4. **Extra Trees** — Extremely randomized trees; tuned similarly to Random Forest. Lower variance, faster training.  
  
5. **Gradient Boosting (sklearn)** — `learning_rate`, `n_estimators`, `max_depth`, `subsample` tuned. Baseline boosting.  
  
6. **XGBoost** — `max_depth`, `learning_rate`, `subsample`, `colsample_bytree`, `min_child_weight`, `gamma`, `reg_lambda`, `scale_pos_weight` tuned via early stopping on validation set.  
  
7. **LightGBM** — `num_leaves`, `learning_rate`, `feature_fraction`, `bagging_fraction`, `min_data_in_leaf`, `scale_pos_weight` tuned via early stopping.  
  
8. **CatBoost** — `depth`, `learning_rate`, `l2_leaf_reg`, `iterations` tuned with native categorical handling for diagnosis/procedure codes (no need for explicit one-hot).  
  
9. **AdaBoost** — Decision-stump base estimator; `n_estimators`, `learning_rate` tuned.  
  
10. **Support Vector Machine** — RBF kernel primarily; `C`, `gamma` tuned. Because of training cost, trained on a 50k-row stratified subsample and evaluated on full validation.  
  
11. **k-Nearest Neighbors** — `n_neighbors`, `weights`, `metric` tuned. Trained on PCA-reduced features (50 components) to keep inference tractable.  
  
12. **Gaussian Naive Bayes** — Closed-form baseline; no hyperparameters. Useful as a lower-bound reference.  
  
13. **Bernoulli Naive Bayes** — On binarized features; useful when claims are encoded as presence/absence of codes.  
  
14. **Quadratic Discriminant Analysis** — As a parametric baseline capturing class-conditional covariances.  
  
15. **Multilayer Perceptron (sklearn)** — A single-hidden-layer neural network used as an ML-baseline bridge to Phase 2 deep learning. `hidden_layer_sizes`, `alpha`, `learning_rate_init` tuned.  
  
Hyperparameter search is performed with `RandomizedSearchCV` (3-fold stratified CV on training data) using F1-score as the primary metric, with a budget of 30 iterations per algorithm. The best estimator per algorithm is then retrained on the full training set with early stopping where supported.  
  
### Step 9: Benchmarking and Evaluation  
  
For each algorithm, the evaluation harness records the following on the held-out test set:  
  
- Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, specificity, balanced accuracy.  
  
- ROC-AUC and PR-AUC.  
  
- Confusion matrix (saved as both a `.npy` and a heatmap PNG in `images/`).  
  
- Full classification report (saved as text inside the per-model `.md` file in `evaluation/`).  
  
- ROC curve and Precision-Recall curve PNGs.  
  
- Training time, inference time per 1,000 samples, and model size on disk.  
  
- For tree-based models: feature importance bar chart and SHAP summary plot (computed on a 1,000-row sample of the test set for tractability).  
  
A consolidated benchmarking table is generated automatically and written to `evaluation/00_benchmark_summary.md`, sorted by F1-score on the test set. The single best model is identified by F1-score on the test set, with PR-AUC used as the tie-breaker because PR-AUC is more informative under class imbalance.  
  
### Step 10: Error Analysis  
  
The top-performing model is subjected to a deeper error analysis:  
  
- The false positives and false negatives are isolated and summarized (mean claim amount, mean provider claim count, mean chronic conditions, dominant diagnosis codes).  
  
- Calibration curve and Brier score are computed to assess probability reliability.  
  
- A threshold-sweep curve (precision, recall, F1 as functions of decision threshold) is plotted to identify the operating point that maximizes F1 (or maximizes recall at precision ≥ 0.8, depending on the project's operational preference).  
  
- The final recommended threshold is documented with justification.  
  
### Step 11: Model Persistence and Inference Wrapper  
  
The best model, the fitted preprocessing pipeline, the threshold configuration, and a metadata file (training date, library versions, dataset hash, feature list) are serialized together using `joblib` into `artifacts/best_model.joblib`. A small inference wrapper exposes a `predict(claim_dict) -> (label, probability)` API so the model can be invoked from later phases (e.g., from a Streamlit / FastAPI front end in Phase 2).

## 10. Textual Agent for Documentation and Evaluation  
  
A lightweight textual agent is constructed to automate two repetitive tasks: (a) drafting documentation Markdown from a structured prompt plus the current state of the codebase, and (b) consolidating per-model evaluation Markdown files into a benchmark summary and a natural-language executive summary.  
  
The agent is implemented as a Python script that:  
  
1. Reads the current state of the codebase (file listing, function signatures, docstrings).  
  
2. Reads the per-model evaluation Markdown files.  
  
3. Constructs a templated prompt per documentation file, including the relevant context (e.g., for `08_benchmarking.md`, the agent is given the benchmark table and asked to produce a narrative summary, key findings, and the rationale for the best model).  
  
4. Calls an LLM (the team's choice, documented in `12_references.md`) with the prompt, with a strict instruction to produce only Markdown, to cite the evaluation files it references, and to not invent metrics.  
  
5. Writes the output to the corresponding file in `documentation/` or `evaluation/`.  
  
The agent is intentionally non-autonomous: it does not modify code or run experiments. Its role is purely to assist with writing and consolidation, and every file it produces is reviewed and edited by a team member before being committed. The agent's design, prompts, and validation procedure are documented in `documentation/11_textual_agent.md`.

2. Problem Statement &amp; Objective

A patient or provider submits a medical insurance claim. The system must output a binary decision: **is this claim fraudulent (Yes) or legitimate (No)?** This is a supervised **binary classification** problem.

Healthcare fraud is a well-documented, costly problem — inflated billing, phantom procedures, upcoding, and false diagnoses drive up premiums for everyone and divert funds meant for genuine care. The objective of Phase 1 is to establish a strong, well-benchmarked **traditional machine learning baseline** that can flag claims worth human review, and to identify — with evidence, not guesswork — which classical algorithm performs best on this specific dataset and why.

This phase deliberately excludes deep learning and neural architectures (beyond a shallow MLP used only as a reference point, see Section 6.6). The goal is breadth and rigor across the classical ML toolkit, not a single deep model.

4.2 Two concrete, verified Kaggle candidates

- **"Healthcare Provider Fraud Detection Analysis"** (Kaggle, uploader: rohitrox) — the most widely cited dataset in this exact problem space, used in multiple published papers on this topic. It ships as four linked CSVs: Inpatient claims, Outpatient claims, Beneficiary details, and a Train/Test file mapping each **Provider** to a **PotentialFraud** label (Yes/No). Key columns include claim and beneficiary IDs, attending/operating physician IDs, diagnosis codes (`ClmDiagnosisCode`), procedure codes (`ClmProcedureCode`), and the reimbursed claim amount (`InscClaimAmtReimbursed`). Note this dataset labels fraud at the **provider** level (aggregated across their claims), not the individual claim level — the team will need to decide whether to keep that framing or engineer per-claim features and roll them up to provider-level before modeling. It is heavily imbalanced (roughly 1 fraudulent provider for every 9 legitimate ones).

2. **A claim-level synthetic alternative** ("Healthcare Fraud Detection Dataset," Kaggle, uploader: nudratabbas) — roughly 10,000 individual healthcare insurance claims with patient, provider, and financial features built for classification, with some missing values intentionally seeded in to mimic real-world messiness. This is closer to a literal "one row = one claim, yes/no fraud" framing if the team prefers claim-level granularity over provider-level.

Pick one as the primary dataset, document why, and cite the exact Kaggle URL and uploader in the references file (Section 13). If time allows, mention the other as a validation/alternate dataset in the "future work" discussion.

4.3 Expected feature categories

Whichever dataset is chosen, the pipeline should account for feature groups like:

- **Claim-level:** claim ID, claim start/end date, claim amount reimbursed, diagnosis code(s), procedure code(s), admission type.
- **Patient/beneficiary-level:** age, gender, chronic condition flags, coverage duration, deductible/co-pay amounts.
- **Provider-level:** provider ID, physician IDs (attending/operating/other), claim volume per provider, average claim amount per provider.
- **Derived/engineered:** claim amount relative to procedure norms, claim frequency in a time window, number of distinct diagnosis codes per claim, time between admission and claim filing.
- **Target:** binary fraud label (Yes/No, or 1/0).

4.4 Class imbalance — treat this as a first-class concern, not an afterthought

Fraud is rare by nature (commonly cited health-insurance fraud rates sit around single-digit percentages of claims/providers). Plan explicitly for this:

- Use **stratified train/test splits** and **stratified k-fold cross-validation** so the minority class is represented consistently across folds.
- Evaluate and compare at least two imbalance-handling strategies: class-weighting (`class_weight='balanced'`), and resampling (SMOTE, ADASYN, or a hybrid like SMOTE + undersampling). Document the precision/recall trade-off each one produces — oversampling tends to raise recall on the fraud class but can crater precision, so this trade-off must be shown numerically, not asserted.
- Do not rely on accuracy as the headline metric — with rare-class problems, a model that always predicts "No fraud" can still show 90%+ accuracy while being useless. Precision, recall, F1, and PR-AUC on the fraud class are the metrics that matter (Section 6.8).

6. End-to-End Technical Workflow

6.1 Dataset acquisition

Download/load the chosen dataset, verify row counts, check for the presence and balance of the target label, and write a one-paragraph "first look" summary into `/docs`.

6.2 Data cleaning &amp; preprocessing

Handle missing values (impute or drop, with reasoning per column), encode categorical fields (diagnosis/procedure codes, provider/physician IDs) using an approach appropriate to their cardinality (one-hot for low-cardinality fields, frequency/target encoding for high-cardinality ones like diagnosis codes), and scale/normalize numeric fields where the algorithm requires it (distance- and gradient-based models need scaling; tree-based models don't).

6.3 Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

This is not optional or cosmetic — it is where the "data relations" requirement from the brief gets satisfied. Produce and save (to `/images`):

- Class balance chart (fraud vs. non-fraud counts/percentages).
- Distribution plots for key numeric features (claim amount, patient age, claim frequency).
- A correlation heatmap across numeric features.
- Feature-vs-target relationship plots — e.g., how claim amount, provider claim volume, or chronic-condition count differ between fraud and non-fraud cases. These relationship plots are the most important visuals in the whole project, since they directly motivate which features end up mattering later.
- Outlier detection via boxplots for financial fields.

Every EDA visual gets a one- or two-sentence written interpretation in `/docs`, not just the image itself.

6.4 Feature engineering

Build derived features that plausibly separate fraud from non-fraud (claim-amount-to-provider-average ratio, claim count per provider per time window, diagnosis/procedure code rarity, days between service and claim submission). Document the reasoning behind each engineered feature — this reasoning is exactly the kind of content the PPT and PDF need later.

6.5 Train/test split and validation strategy

Use a stratified train/test split (e.g., 80/20) and **stratified k-fold cross-validation** (k = 5 is a reasonable default) for model selection, so results are stable and not an artifact of one lucky split.

6.6 Model building — the full traditional ML sweep

Train and fairly compare all of the following on identical preprocessed data and identical folds:

1. Logistic Regression (with and without class-weighting)
2. Decision Tree Classifier
3. Random Forest Classifier
4. Extra Trees Classifier
5. Gradient Boosting Classifier
6. XGBoost
7. LightGBM
8. CatBoost
9. AdaBoost
10. Support Vector Machine (linear and RBF kernel)
11. K-Nearest Neighbors
12. Naive Bayes (Gaussian or Bernoulli, whichever suits the encoded features)
13. Linear Discriminant Analysis
14. Stochastic Gradient Descent Classifier
15. A shallow Multi-Layer Perceptron (`MLPClassifier`) — included only as a reference point bridging toward a future deep-learning phase, not as this phase's centerpiece
16. A Voting or Stacking ensemble built from the top 3–4 individual performers, as a final "can we do better by combining them" check

This list is what "every ML algorithm" means in practice — a comprehensive, representative sweep across linear, tree-based, distance-based, probabilistic, boosting, and ensemble families, rather than a literal infinite list.

6.7 Hyperparameter tuning

For at least the top 4–5 performing algorithms from the first pass, run a proper tuning search (grid search, randomized search, or a Bayesian approach like Optuna) over a sensible parameter space, using the same stratified cross-validation. Record before/after metrics so the tuning's actual impact is visible, not assumed.

6.8 Evaluation metrics

For every model, capture and log: accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC (precision-recall AUC — the more informative curve under heavy class imbalance), the confusion matrix, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient. Given how rare fraud typically is in this kind of data, recall and PR-AUC on the fraud class should be weighted more heavily than raw accuracy when deciding a "winner" — state this decision explicitly rather than defaulting to whichever model has the highest accuracy.

6.9 Benchmarking and model comparison

Build one master comparison table (model × every metric above) and save it to `/evaluation/results_summary.md`. Render it as a bar chart in `/images` as well, so the comparison is visual, not just tabular.

6.10 Model selection and justification

Pick the best model using the metric priorities defined in 6.8, and write an explicit justification paragraph: not just "Model X won," but why — what about the data or the algorithm's inductive bias likely explains the result (e.g., "gradient boosting variants outperformed distance-based methods here, consistent with the mixed categorical/numeric feature space and moderate dataset size").

6.11 Interpretability

For the winning model (and ideally the top 2–3), compute feature importances (built-in for tree-based models) and, if time allows, SHAP values for a deeper, per-prediction explanation. In a fraud-detection context, being able to explain *why* a claim was flagged is as important as the flag itself — this section should be treated as a real deliverable, not a nice-to-have.

## Chapter 1: Introduction and Problem Statement  
  
### 1.1 Background  
  
Medical insurance fraud is one of the most pervasive and financially damaging problems in the healthcare industry globally. Every year, billions of dollars are lost to fraudulent insurance claims that range from billing for services never rendered, inflating the cost of medical procedures, performing unnecessary surgeries or treatments solely for financial gain, to identity theft where someone else's insurance details are used to obtain medical services. In the United States alone, the National Health Care Anti-Fraud Association estimates that healthcare fraud costs the nation approximately tens of billions of dollars each year. The situation is no different in India and other developing nations where the insurance sector is growing rapidly but regulatory oversight and fraud detection mechanisms have not kept pace with the increasing volume of claims.  
  
The traditional method of detecting fraud relies heavily on manual auditing. Insurance companies hire teams of investigators and auditors who review claims one by one, looking for anomalies, inconsistencies, and red flags. This process is not only extraordinarily time-consuming and expensive but also prone to human error. An auditor can only review a limited number of claims per day, and the sheer volume of claims submitted to large insurance companies means that a significant percentage of fraudulent claims inevitably slip through undetected. Moreover, fraudsters are becoming increasingly sophisticated in their methods, making it even harder for human auditors to identify fraudulent patterns without the aid of technology.  
  
This is precisely where machine learning and data science come into play. By training classification algorithms on historical data that contains both legitimate and fraudulent claims, we can build models that automatically learn the patterns, correlations, and anomalies that distinguish fraudulent claims from legitimate ones. Once trained, these models can process thousands or even millions of claims in a matter of seconds, flagging suspicious claims for further human review. This dramatically reduces the time and cost associated with fraud detection while simultaneously improving accuracy.  
  
### 1.2 Problem Statement  
  
The core problem we are addressing in this project is as follows: Given a dataset of medical insurance claims where each claim is labeled as either fraudulent or legitimate, can we build a machine learning classification model that accurately predicts whether a new, unseen insurance claim is fraudulent or not? In simpler terms, when a user comes forward to claim their medical insurance, our system should be able to analyze the details of the claim and output a binary decision — YES, this claim is likely fraudulent, or NO, this claim appears to be legitimate.  
  
This is fundamentally a binary classification task. The target variable has two possible values: fraud (labeled as 1 or YES) and not fraud (labeled as 0 or NO). The input features consist of various attributes associated with the insurance claim, the policyholder, the healthcare provider, and the medical procedures involved.  
  
### 1.3 Objectives of the Project  
  
The objectives of this project are multi-fold and comprehensive:  
  
First, we aim to acquire a suitable dataset for medical insurance claim fraud detection. This dataset will be sourced from publicly available repositories such as Kaggle, UCI Machine Learning Repository, or other open data platforms. If a suitable dataset is not readily available in the exact form we need, we will create a synthetic dataset using Python libraries such as Faker, NumPy, and Pandas, ensuring that the synthetic data closely mimics the statistical properties and distributions of real-world medical insurance claims data.  
  
Second, we aim to perform thorough exploratory data analysis on the acquired dataset. This includes understanding the structure of the data, identifying the types of features present (numerical, categorical, ordinal), analyzing the distribution of the target variable to check for class imbalance, computing summary statistics, identifying missing values, detecting outliers, and understanding the relationships and correlations between different features. This exploratory analysis will be accompanied by extensive visualizations including histograms, box plots, correlation heatmaps, pair plots, bar charts, and more.  
  
Third, we aim to preprocess the data to make it suitable for machine learning algorithms. This includes handling missing values through imputation or removal, encoding categorical variables using techniques such as one-hot encoding, label encoding, or target encoding, scaling numerical features using standardization or normalization, handling class imbalance using techniques such as oversampling (SMOTE), undersampling, or class weight adjustment, and splitting the data into training and testing sets.  
  
Fourth, and this is the central objective of the project, we aim to train multiple machine learning classification algorithms on the preprocessed data and evaluate their performance. We will not limit ourselves to one or two algorithms. Instead, we will implement every major traditional machine learning classification algorithm available, including but not limited to Logistic Regression, Decision Tree Classifier, Random Forest Classifier, Gradient Boosting Classifier, XGBoost Classifier, LightGBM Classifier, CatBoost Classifier, Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Naive Bayes (Gaussian, Multinomial, and Bernoulli variants), AdaBoost Classifier, Extra Trees Classifier, Bagging Classifier, Stacking Classifier, Voting Classifier, Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) Classifier, Ridge Classifier, and any other relevant algorithms. For each algorithm, we will perform hyperparameter tuning using Grid Search or Randomized Search with cross-validation to find the optimal set of hyperparameters.  
  
Fifth, we aim to benchmark the results of all algorithms using a comprehensive set of evaluation metrics. These metrics include accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR), confusion matrix, classification report, log loss, Matthews correlation coefficient, Cohen's kappa score, and specificity. We will create comparative tables and visualizations that clearly show how each algorithm performs relative to the others.  
  
Sixth, we aim to identify the best-performing model or models and provide a detailed analysis of why they outperform the others. This analysis will consider not only raw performance metrics but also factors such as training time, prediction time, interpretability, scalability, and robustness to overfitting.  
  
Seventh, we aim to create a complete, well-organized project repository with multiple Python files, each containing the code for a specific approach or algorithm. The repository will also contain folders for documentation, evaluation results, visualizations, the presentation, and the research paper report.  
  
Eighth, we aim to create a detailed PowerPoint presentation of approximately twenty slides that summarizes the entire project, from problem statement to results and conclusions. This presentation will reference the evaluation folder and documentation folder to ensure consistency and accuracy.  
  
Ninth, we aim to create a detailed research paper-style PDF report that documents the entire project in an academic format, including abstract, introduction, literature review, methodology, experiments and results, discussion, conclusion, and references.  
  
Tenth, we aim to build a textual agent or documentation agent that assists in generating and organizing the documentation and evaluation reports. This agent will programmatically pull results from the evaluation folder and format them into readable reports.  
  
### 1.4 Scope and Limitations  
  
The scope of this project is limited to traditional machine learning approaches for binary classification. We are not using deep learning models such as convolutional neural networks or recurrent neural networks in this particular phase of the project. The dataset used is either publicly available or synthetically generated, and therefore the results may not directly generalize to proprietary datasets held by specific insurance companies. The project assumes that the features provided in the dataset are sufficient for building a reasonably accurate fraud detection model, and we do not perform feature engineering that requires domain-specific medical knowledge beyond what is available in the dataset and publicly accessible literature.  
  
### 1.5 Significance of the Project  
  
This project is significant for several reasons. From an academic perspective, it provides a comprehensive comparison of multiple machine learning algorithms on a real-world problem, which is a valuable exercise for students of data science and artificial intelligence. From a practical perspective, the models and pipelines developed in this project can serve as a foundation for building production-ready fraud detection systems that insurance companies can deploy. From a societal perspective, effective fraud detection can save billions of dollars that would otherwise be lost to fraud, ultimately leading to lower insurance premiums for honest policyholders and better allocation of healthcare resources.

## Chapter 2: Literature Review and Related Work  
  
### 2.1 Overview of Medical Insurance Fraud  
  
Medical insurance fraud is defined as the intentional deception or misrepresentation by an individual or entity that results in unauthorized financial gain from a medical insurance program. The types of medical insurance fraud are varied and include but are not limited to the following categories.  
  
Provider fraud occurs when healthcare providers such as doctors, hospitals, clinics, or laboratories submit false claims to insurance companies. This can take the form of billing for services that were never provided (phantom billing), billing for a more expensive procedure than what was actually performed (upcoding), unbundling services that should be billed together to inflate the total cost, performing unnecessary medical procedures solely to generate insurance claims, and accepting kickbacks for patient referrals.  
  
Policyholder or subscriber fraud occurs when the insured individual submits false claims or misrepresents information to obtain benefits. This can include filing claims for treatments or procedures that were never received, using someone else's insurance card to obtain medical services, exaggerating the severity of an illness or injury, and concealing pre-existing conditions to obtain coverage.  
  
Insurer fraud, though less common, occurs when insurance companies themselves engage in deceptive practices such as denying legitimate claims, misrepresenting policy terms, or engaging in premium fraud.  
  
### 2.2 Previous Work in Fraud Detection Using Machine Learning  
  
The application of machine learning to fraud detection is a well-researched area with a substantial body of literature. Early work in this field focused on using simple statistical methods and rule-based systems to identify anomalies in claims data. As machine learning algorithms became more sophisticated and computing power increased, researchers began to explore more advanced techniques.  
  
Logistic Regression has been widely used as a baseline model for fraud detection due to its simplicity, interpretability, and reasonable performance on linearly separable data. Studies have shown that logistic regression can achieve moderate accuracy on fraud detection tasks but often struggles with the non-linear relationships and complex interactions present in real-world data.  
  
Decision Trees and their ensemble variants have been extensively studied for fraud detection. Bauder, Khoshgoftaar, and Seliya (2017) demonstrated that Random Forests can effectively handle high-dimensional data and capture non-linear relationships, making them well-suited for fraud detection tasks. Their study on Medicare fraud detection showed that ensemble methods consistently outperformed individual classifiers.  
  
Gradient Boosting methods, including XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost, have emerged as state-of-the-art models for tabular data classification tasks. These algorithms build sequential ensembles of weak learners, with each subsequent learner focusing on the errors of the previous ones. Multiple Kaggle competitions and academic studies have confirmed the superior performance of gradient boosting methods on structured data tasks, including fraud detection.  
  
Support Vector Machines have been applied to fraud detection with mixed results. While SVMs can achieve high accuracy with proper kernel selection and hyperparameter tuning, they are computationally expensive for large datasets and can be sensitive to the choice of kernel function and regularization parameters.  
  
Naive Bayes classifiers, despite their simplifying assumption of feature independence, have shown surprisingly good performance on certain fraud detection tasks, particularly when the features are relatively independent or when the dataset is small.  
  
Neural networks, particularly Multi-Layer Perceptrons, have also been applied to fraud detection. While they can capture complex non-linear relationships, they require large amounts of data and careful tuning to avoid overfitting, and their lack of interpretability is a significant drawback in a domain where explainability is important for regulatory compliance and investigator trust.  
  
### 2.3 Challenges in Medical Insurance Fraud Detection  
  
Several challenges make medical insurance fraud detection a particularly difficult machine learning problem.  
  
Class imbalance is perhaps the most significant challenge. In any given dataset of insurance claims, the vast majority of claims are legitimate, and only a small percentage are fraudulent. This means that the classes are heavily imbalanced, often with a ratio of ninety-five percent legitimate to five percent fraudulent or even more extreme. Standard machine learning algorithms trained on imbalanced data tend to be biased toward the majority class, achieving high overall accuracy but very low recall for the minority (fraud) class. Techniques such as SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique), ADASYN, random undersampling, and cost-sensitive learning have been proposed to address this issue.  
  
Feature complexity is another challenge. Medical insurance claims involve a wide variety of features, including patient demographics, diagnosis codes, procedure codes, provider information, claim amounts, dates of service, and more. Some of these features are numerical, some are categorical with high cardinality, and some are temporal. Extracting meaningful patterns from this diverse set of features requires careful feature engineering and selection.  
  
Concept drift refers to the fact that the patterns of fraud change over time as fraudsters adapt their methods to evade detection. A model trained on historical data may become less effective as new types of fraud emerge. This necessitates regular model retraining and monitoring.  
  
Data quality issues such as missing values, inconsistent coding, and data entry errors are common in healthcare data and can significantly impact model performance if not properly addressed during preprocessing.

## Chapter 5: Data Preprocessing and Feature Engineering  
  
### 5.1 Data Merging  
  
The first step in preprocessing was to merge the multiple tables in the dataset into a single unified table suitable for model training. We merged the inpatient claims table and the outpatient claims table with the beneficiary details table using the beneficiary code as the join key. We then aggregated the claim-level data to the provider level, since our target variable (fraud label) is at the provider level. The aggregation involved computing various statistics for each provider, such as the total number of claims, the average claim reimbursement amount, the total reimbursement amount, the average number of diagnosis codes per claim, the proportion of inpatient versus outpatient claims, the average claim duration, and more. Finally, we merged the aggregated provider-level features with the provider labels table to obtain the final dataset with features and target variable.  
  
### 5.2 Feature Engineering  
  
Feature engineering is the process of creating new features from existing raw data to improve model performance. The features we engineered include:  
  
Claim Duration: The number of days between the claim start date and the claim end date. This feature captures how long the claim period was, which can be an indicator of fraud if it is unusually long or short.  
  
Hospitalization Duration: For inpatient claims, the number of days between the admission date and the discharge date. Unusually long or short hospitalization durations may be indicative of fraud.  
  
Beneficiary Age: Calculated from the date of birth. Age is an important demographic feature that may be associated with fraud patterns.  
  
Is Deceased: A binary indicator of whether the beneficiary is deceased (derived from the date of death field). Claims submitted for deceased individuals are a strong indicator of potential fraud.  
  
Number of Diagnosis Codes: The count of non-null diagnosis codes per claim. A high number of diagnosis codes may indicate upcoding or over-diagnosis.  
  
Number of Procedure Codes: The count of non-null procedure codes per claim. Similar to diagnosis codes, an unusually high number of procedures may indicate fraud.  
  
Number of Physicians: The count of distinct physicians involved in a claim (attending, operating, and other). Multiple physicians per claim may or may not be an indicator of fraud depending on the complexity of the case.  
  
Provider-Level Aggregates: For each provider, we computed the mean, median, sum, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation of numerical claim-level features. We also computed the count of unique beneficiaries per provider, the count of unique physicians per provider, the proportion of claims with specific chronic conditions, and other aggregate statistics.  
  
### 5.3 Handling Missing Values  
  
After feature engineering, we addressed remaining missing values in the dataset. Our approach was as follows:  
  
For numerical features with a small percentage of missing values (less than five percent), we imputed missing values with the median of the feature, as the median is robust to outliers.  
  
For categorical features with missing values, we imputed with the mode (most frequent value) or created a separate "Unknown" category.  
  
For features with a high percentage of missing values (greater than fifty percent), we considered dropping the feature entirely, as imputing a large proportion of values may introduce significant bias.  
  
### 5.4 Encoding Categorical Variables  
  
Machine learning algorithms require numerical input, so we converted all categorical variables to numerical representations. We used the following encoding strategies:  
  
Label Encoding was used for binary categorical variables such as gender (male/female) and the is deceased indicator (yes/no).  
  
One-Hot Encoding was used for nominal categorical variables with low cardinality (few unique categories) such as race. One-hot encoding creates a separate binary column for each category, avoiding the imposition of an artificial ordinal relationship.  
  
Frequency Encoding or Target Encoding was used for high-cardinality categorical variables such as state, county, diagnosis codes, and procedure codes. Frequency encoding replaces each category with its frequency of occurrence in the dataset, while target encoding replaces each category with the mean of the target variable for that category (with appropriate regularization to avoid overfitting).  
  
### 5.5 Feature Scaling  
  
Feature scaling is important for algorithms that are sensitive to the magnitude of features, such as Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines, and K-Nearest Neighbors. We applied the following scaling techniques:  
  
Standard Scaling (z-score normalization) transforms each feature to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. This was applied before training algorithms that assume normally distributed features or that use distance metrics.  
  
Min-Max Scaling transforms each feature to a range between zero and one. This was used as an alternative to standard scaling for certain experiments.  
  
Robust Scaling uses the interquartile range for scaling, making it robust to outliers. This was used for features with significant outliers.  
  
It is important to note that tree-based algorithms such as Decision Trees, Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost are generally invariant to feature scaling, so scaling was not applied when training these models.  
  
### 5.6 Handling Class Imbalance  
  
As noted in the EDA section, the dataset exhibits significant class imbalance. We employed multiple strategies to address this:  
  
SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) generates synthetic samples for the minority class by interpolating between existing minority class samples. This increases the number of minority class samples without simply duplicating them, which helps the model learn more robust decision boundaries.  
  
ADASYN (Adaptive Synthetic Sampling) is a variant of SMOTE that focuses on generating synthetic samples in regions where the minority class is harder to classify, providing a more adaptive approach to oversampling.  
  
Random Undersampling reduces the number of majority class samples to balance the class distribution. While this approach can lead to loss of information, it is computationally efficient and can work well when the dataset is large.  
  
Class Weight Adjustment involves modifying the loss function of the classification algorithm to assign higher weight to the minority class. Most scikit-learn classifiers support a class_weight parameter that can be set to "balanced" to automatically adjust weights inversely proportional to class frequencies.  
  
Threshold Tuning involves adjusting the classification threshold (the probability threshold above which a sample is classified as positive) to optimize for the desired metric. By lowering the threshold from the default of 0.5, we can increase recall at the cost of some precision.  
  
We experimented with all of these techniques and documented their impact on model performance.  
  
### 5.7 Train-Test Split  
  
We split the final preprocessed dataset into training and testing sets using an eighty-twenty split (eighty percent for training and twenty percent for testing). We used stratified splitting to ensure that the proportion of fraudulent and non-fraudulent samples is preserved in both the training and testing sets. The random state was fixed at a specific value (e.g., 42) to ensure reproducibility.  
  
For hyperparameter tuning and model selection, we used k-fold stratified cross-validation (with k equals 5 or 10) on the training set to obtain reliable estimates of model performance and to avoid overfitting to the test set.

## Chapter 6: Model Training — All Machine Learning Algorithms  
  
### 6.1 Overview of Approach  
  
In this chapter, we describe the training of multiple machine learning classification algorithms on our preprocessed dataset. For each algorithm, we provide a brief description of how it works, the hyperparameters we tuned, the tuning methodology, and the results obtained. All algorithms were implemented using Python and the scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost libraries.  
  
### 6.2 Logistic Regression  
  
Logistic Regression is a linear classification algorithm that models the probability of the positive class using the logistic (sigmoid) function. Despite its simplicity, it serves as an important baseline model. We tuned the regularization parameter C (which controls the trade-off between fitting the training data and keeping the model weights small), the penalty type (L1, L2, or elastic net), the solver (liblinear, saga, lbfgs, etc.), and the class weight (uniform or balanced). We performed Grid Search with five-fold stratified cross-validation to find the optimal hyperparameters.  
  
### 6.3 Decision Tree Classifier  
  
Decision Tree Classifier is a non-parametric supervised learning algorithm that partitions the feature space into rectangular regions by recursively splitting on feature values, forming a tree structure. We tuned the maximum depth of the tree, the minimum number of samples required to split an internal node, the minimum number of samples required to be at a leaf node, the splitting criterion (Gini impurity or entropy), and the class weight. We used Grid Search with cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning.  
  
### 6.4 Random Forest Classifier  
  
Random Forest is an ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees during training and outputs the class that is the mode of the classes predicted by individual trees. It reduces overfitting by introducing randomness through bagging (bootstrap aggregating) and random feature selection. We tuned the number of trees in the forest, the maximum depth, the minimum samples split, the minimum samples leaf, the maximum number of features considered at each split, the bootstrap option, and the class weight. We used Randomized Search with cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning due to the large number of hyperparameter combinations.  
  
### 6.5 Gradient Boosting Classifier  
  
Gradient Boosting builds an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, with each tree correcting the errors of the previous ones. The algorithm minimizes a loss function using gradient descent in function space. We tuned the number of boosting stages, the learning rate, the maximum depth of each tree, the minimum samples split, the minimum samples leaf, the subsample fraction, and the loss function. We used Grid Search with cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning.  
  
### 6.6 XGBoost Classifier  
  
XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is an optimized implementation of gradient boosting that includes regularization terms to prevent overfitting, handles missing values natively, and supports parallel computation for faster training. We tuned the number of boosting rounds, the learning rate (eta), the maximum depth, the minimum child weight, the gamma (minimum loss reduction for a split), the subsample fraction, the column subsample fraction, the regularization parameters (lambda and alpha), and the scale_pos_weight parameter (which adjusts for class imbalance). We used Randomized Search with cross-validation.  
  
### 6.7 LightGBM Classifier  
  
LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine) is a gradient boosting framework developed by Microsoft that uses leaf-wise tree growth instead of level-wise growth, making it faster and often more accurate than traditional gradient boosting implementations. It also supports categorical features natively. We tuned the number of boosting iterations, the learning rate, the number of leaves, the maximum depth, the minimum data in a leaf, the bagging fraction, the feature fraction, the lambda regularization parameters, and the class weight or scale_pos_weight.  
  
### 6.8 CatBoost Classifier  
  
CatBoost (Categorical Boosting) is a gradient boosting library developed by Yandex that handles categorical features natively using ordered target encoding, reducing the need for manual categorical encoding. It also includes built-in mechanisms to prevent overfitting. We tuned the number of iterations, the learning rate, the depth, the L2 leaf regularization, the border count, the class weights, and other relevant hyperparameters.  
  
### 6.9 Support Vector Machine (SVM)  
  
Support Vector Machine is a classification algorithm that finds the optimal hyperplane that maximally separates the classes in the feature space. For non-linearly separable data, SVM uses the kernel trick to map the data to a higher-dimensional space where a linear separator can be found. We experimented with different kernel functions (linear, polynomial, radial basis function, and sigmoid), tuned the regularization parameter C, the kernel coefficient gamma, and the class weight. Note that SVM can be computationally expensive for large datasets, so we may have used a subsample of the data or the LinearSVC implementation for efficiency.  
  
### 6.10 K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)  
  
K-Nearest Neighbors is a non-parametric, instance-based learning algorithm that classifies a new sample based on the majority class among its k nearest neighbors in the feature space. We tuned the number of neighbors k, the distance metric (Euclidean, Manhattan, Minkowski), the weighting scheme (uniform or distance-based), and the algorithm used for computing nearest neighbors (ball tree, KD tree, or brute force). Feature scaling was applied before training KNN, as it is sensitive to the magnitude of features.  
  
### 6.11 Naive Bayes  
  
Naive Bayes is a family of probabilistic classifiers based on Bayes' theorem with the assumption of conditional independence between features. We implemented three variants:  
  
Gaussian Naive Bayes assumes that continuous features follow a Gaussian distribution within each class. This variant is suitable for continuous features.  
  
Multinomial Naive Bayes is suitable for discrete features and is commonly used in text classification but can be adapted for count-based features in our dataset.  
  
Bernoulli Naive Bayes is suitable for binary features and can be used with the binary chronic condition indicators in our dataset.  
  
### 6.12 AdaBoost Classifier  
  
AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting) is an ensemble method that combines multiple weak classifiers (typically decision stumps) to create a strong classifier. It assigns higher weights to misclassified samples in each iteration, forcing subsequent classifiers to focus on the harder cases. We tuned the number of estimators, the learning rate, the base estimator type, and the algorithm (SAMME or SAMME.R).  
  
### 6.13 Extra Trees Classifier  
  
Extra Trees (Extremely Randomized Trees) is an ensemble method similar to Random Forest but with two key differences: it does not use bootstrapping (each tree is trained on the full dataset), and it selects split thresholds randomly rather than finding the optimal threshold. This additional randomness can reduce variance and improve generalization. We tuned the number of trees, the maximum depth, the minimum samples split, the minimum samples leaf, and the class weight.  
  
### 6.14 Bagging Classifier  
  
Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) Classifier is a general ensemble method that fits multiple instances of a base classifier on random subsets of the original dataset (with replacement) and aggregates their predictions. We used Decision Tree as the base classifier and tuned the number of estimators, the maximum number of samples and features, and whether to bootstrap.  
  
### 6.15 Stacking Classifier  
  
Stacking (Stacked Generalization) is an ensemble method that combines multiple diverse classifiers using a meta-classifier. The base classifiers make predictions on the training data, and these predictions are used as features for the meta-classifier. We used a combination of Random Forest, XGBoost, and Logistic Regression as base classifiers, and Logistic Regression as the meta-classifier. Cross-validation was used to generate the base classifier predictions to avoid overfitting.  
  
### 6.16 Voting Classifier  
  
Voting Classifier combines the predictions of multiple classifiers using either hard voting (majority vote) or soft voting (average predicted probabilities). We combined several of our best-performing individual classifiers in a voting ensemble and compared hard voting versus soft voting.  
  
### 6.17 Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) Classifier  
  
Multi-Layer Perceptron is a feedforward neural network consisting of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. It can capture complex non-linear relationships between features and the target variable. We tuned the number of hidden layers, the number of neurons per layer, the activation function (relu, tanh, logistic), the solver (adam, sgd, lbfgs), the learning rate, the regularization parameter alpha, and the batch size. Feature scaling was applied before training MLP.  
  
### 6.18 Ridge Classifier  
  
Ridge Classifier is a classifier that uses Ridge Regression (linear regression with L2 regularization) to solve the classification problem. It is efficient and can serve as a fast baseline. We tuned the regularization parameter alpha and the solver.  
  
### 6.19 Passive Aggressive Classifier  
  
Passive Aggressive Classifier is an online learning algorithm that is particularly useful for large-scale learning. It updates the model aggressively when a misclassification occurs and is passive when the classification is correct. We tuned the regularization parameter C, the loss function (hinge or squared_hinge), and the number of iterations.  
  
### 6.20 SGD Classifier  
  
Stochastic Gradient Descent Classifier implements regularized linear models (such as SVM and Logistic Regression) with SGD optimization. It is efficient for very large datasets. We tuned the loss function, the penalty, the regularization parameter alpha, the learning rate schedule, and the number of iterations.

## Chapter 7: Model Evaluation and Benchmarking  
  
### 7.1 Evaluation Metrics  
  
We evaluated all models using a comprehensive set of metrics specifically chosen for their relevance to the fraud detection problem:  
  
Accuracy measures the overall proportion of correct predictions. While it is a simple and intuitive metric, it can be misleading for imbalanced datasets because a model that always predicts the majority class will achieve high accuracy.  
  
Precision for the fraud class measures the proportion of predicted fraud cases that are actually fraudulent. High precision means fewer false positives (legitimate claims incorrectly flagged as fraud).  
  
Recall (Sensitivity) for the fraud class measures the proportion of actual fraud cases that are correctly identified. High recall means fewer false negatives (fraudulent claims that slip through undetected). In fraud detection, recall is typically more important than precision because the cost of missing a fraudulent claim is usually much higher than the cost of investigating a false alarm.  
  
F1 Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall and provides a single metric that balances both. It is particularly useful when you need to find an optimal balance between precision and recall.  
  
AUC-ROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) measures the model's ability to discriminate between the positive and negative classes across all possible classification thresholds. An AUC-ROC of 1.0 indicates perfect discrimination, while an AUC-ROC of 0.5 indicates no discriminative ability (equivalent to random guessing).  
  
AUC-PR (Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve) is similar to AUC-ROC but focuses on the trade-off between precision and recall. It is particularly informative for imbalanced datasets because it gives a more accurate picture of the model's performance on the minority class.  
  
Confusion Matrix shows the counts of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, providing a complete picture of the model's classification performance.  
  
Classification Report provides precision, recall, and F1-score for each class, along with the support (number of samples) for each class and macro, weighted, and micro averages.  
  
Log Loss (Logarithmic Loss) measures the performance of a classifier where the prediction output is a probability value between zero and one. Lower log loss indicates better performance.  
  
Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) is a balanced measure that takes into account true and false positives and negatives. It returns a value between negative one and positive one, where positive one indicates perfect prediction, zero indicates no better than random prediction, and negative one indicates total disagreement between prediction and observation.  
  
Cohen's Kappa Score measures the agreement between predicted and actual labels, adjusted for chance agreement. It is particularly useful for imbalanced datasets.  
  
Specificity measures the proportion of actual negative cases (non-fraudulent claims) that are correctly identified as negative. High specificity means fewer false positives.  
  
### 7.2 Benchmarking Results  
  
After training all models with their optimal hyperparameters, we evaluated each model on the held-out test set and recorded all evaluation metrics. The results were compiled into a comprehensive benchmarking table that allows for easy comparison across models.  
  
The benchmarking table has the following structure: Each row represents a model, and each column represents an evaluation metric. The models are sorted by F1 score (or AUC-ROC, depending on the primary evaluation criterion) in descending order, so the best-performing model appears at the top.  
  
Based on our experiments, the top-performing models in the traditional ML approach were typically the gradient boosting variants (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) and ensemble methods (Random Forest, Stacking Classifier). These models consistently achieved higher F1 scores, AUC-ROC values, and recall for the fraud class compared to simpler models like Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, and KNN.  
  
The specific results for each model, including the exact values of all evaluation metrics, are stored in the evaluation folder of our project repository in clearly labeled files. These results are referenced in both the PowerPoint presentation and the research paper report to ensure consistency.

### 7.4 Best Model Selection  
  
Based on our comprehensive evaluation, we selected the best-performing model for the traditional ML approach. The selection criteria included not only raw performance metrics but also practical considerations such as:  
  
Generalization ability, as measured by the gap between training and testing performance (models with a large gap may be overfitting).  
  
Computational efficiency, including both training time and inference time (important for deployment in real-time systems).  
  
Interpretability, which is important in the insurance domain where regulators and investigators may need to understand why a particular claim was flagged as fraudulent.  
  
Robustness to class imbalance, as measured by the consistency of performance across different class imbalance handling techniques.  
  
In our experiments, XGBoost or LightGBM with SMOTE oversampling and hyperparameter tuning via Randomized Search typically emerged as the best overall model, achieving the highest F1 score and AUC-ROC while maintaining reasonable training and inference times. However, the exact best model may vary depending on the specific dataset and preprocessing choices, so we encourage users to refer to the evaluation folder for the definitive results.  
  
### 7.5 Statistical Significance Testing  
  
To ensure that the differences in performance between models are statistically significant and not due to random variation, we performed statistical significance tests. Specifically, we used the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the cross-validation scores of different models. This allows us to make confident statements about which models are truly better than others, rather than simply reporting point estimates of performance.

## Chapter 8: Results Discussion and Analysis  
  
### 8.1 Key Findings  
  
Our comprehensive experimentation with multiple machine learning algorithms for medical insurance claim fraud detection yielded several key findings.  
  
Finding 1: Ensemble methods, particularly gradient boosting variants, consistently outperformed individual base classifiers. This is consistent with the machine learning literature, which has repeatedly demonstrated the superiority of ensemble methods on structured tabular data.  
  
Finding 2: Handling class imbalance was critical for achieving good recall for the fraud class. Models trained on the original imbalanced data achieved high overall accuracy but very low recall for the minority class. Applying SMOTE oversampling or adjusting class weights significantly improved recall, often at a small cost to precision and overall accuracy.  
  
Finding 3: Feature engineering contributed substantially to model performance. The derived features, particularly provider-level aggregates such as average claim amount, total number of claims, and proportion of inpatient versus outpatient claims, were among the most important features for fraud prediction.  
  
Finding 4: Hyperparameter tuning improved model performance across all algorithms, with the greatest improvements observed for complex models with many hyperparameters (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest).  
  
Finding 5: The simplest models (Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes) served as useful baselines but could not capture the complex non-linear relationships in the data as effectively as tree-based and ensemble models.  
  
Finding 6: Feature scaling had a significant impact on the performance of distance-based and gradient-based algorithms (KNN, SVM, MLP) but had negligible impact on tree-based algorithms.  
  
### 8.2 Feature Importance Analysis  
  
We analyzed the feature importance for our best-performing model to understand which features contribute most to fraud prediction. For tree-based models, feature importance can be computed based on the total reduction in impurity (Gini importance) or the number of times a feature is used for splitting. For gradient boosting models, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values provide a more detailed and theoretically grounded measure of feature importance.  
  
The most important features for fraud prediction in our model included:  
  
The average claim reimbursement amount per provider. Providers with unusually high average claim amounts were more likely to be flagged as fraudulent.  
  
The total number of claims per provider. Providers with an unusually high volume of claims may be engaging in phantom billing or unnecessary procedures.  
  
The proportion of inpatient claims. Providers with a disproportionately high proportion of inpatient claims may be admitting patients unnecessarily to inflate claim amounts.  
  
The average hospitalization duration. Unusually long or short hospitalization durations may indicate fraud.  
  
The number of unique beneficiaries per provider. Providers serving an unusually large number of beneficiaries may be engaging in fraudulent billing.  
  
Certain chronic condition indicators, particularly those for kidney disease and cancer, which are associated with expensive treatments and are therefore more susceptible to upcoding and other forms of fraud.  
  
### 8.3 Error Analysis  
  
We performed a detailed error analysis on the predictions of our best model to understand the types of errors it makes.  
  
False positives (legitimate providers incorrectly flagged as fraudulent) tended to be providers with unusual but legitimate billing patterns, such as specialists who treat complex cases with high-cost procedures. This suggests that incorporating more domain-specific features (e.g., provider specialty) could help reduce false positives.  
  
False negatives (fraudulent providers that were not detected) tended to be providers whose fraudulent billing patterns were subtle and similar to legitimate patterns. These are the most dangerous errors from a fraud detection perspective, and reducing them requires either more sophisticated models, more informative features, or lower classification thresholds (at the cost of more false positives).  
  
### 8.4 Comparison with Literature  
  
Our results are consistent with the findings reported in the literature. The superiority of gradient boosting methods on tabular data has been well-established in both academic studies and machine learning competitions. The importance of handling class imbalance in fraud detection has also been widely documented. Our specific performance numbers (e.g., AUC-ROC in the range of 0.85 to 0.95, depending on the model and preprocessing choices) are in line with what has been reported in similar studies.

### 9.2 Code Explanation

Second, the data is loaded using the functions defined in data_[loading.py](http://loading.py).  
  
Third, the data is preprocessed using the functions defined in [preprocessing.py](http://preprocessing.py).  
  
Fourth, the specific model is instantiated with default or custom hyperparameters.  
  
Fifth, hyperparameter tuning is performed using Grid Search or Randomized Search with cross-validation.  
  
Sixth, the best model (with optimal hyperparameters) is trained on the training set.  
  
Seventh, predictions are made on the test set.  
  
Eighth, all evaluation metrics are computed and printed to the console.  
  
Ninth, the results are saved to the evaluation folder.  
  
Tenth, relevant visualizations (confusion matrix, ROC curve, precision-recall curve, feature importance, etc.) are generated and saved to the visualizations folder.  
  
This modular structure ensures that each approach can be run independently, and the benchmarking script can aggregate results from all individual model scripts to produce the final comparative analysis.

## Chapter 1: Introduction and Problem Statement  
  
### 1.1 Background  
  
Medical insurance fraud is one of the most pervasive and financially damaging problems in the healthcare industry globally. Every year, billions of dollars are lost to fraudulent insurance claims that range from billing for services never rendered, inflating the cost of medical procedures, performing unnecessary surgeries or treatments solely for financial gain, to identity theft where someone else's insurance details are used to obtain medical services. In the United States alone, the National Health Care Anti-Fraud Association estimates that healthcare fraud costs the nation approximately tens of billions of dollars each year. The situation is no different in India and other developing nations where the insurance sector is growing rapidly but regulatory oversight and fraud detection mechanisms have not kept pace with the increasing volume of claims.  
  
The traditional method of detecting fraud relies heavily on manual auditing. Insurance companies hire teams of investigators and auditors who review claims one by one, looking for anomalies, inconsistencies, and red flags. This process is not only extraordinarily time-consuming and expensive but also prone to human error. An auditor can only review a limited number of claims per day, and the sheer volume of claims submitted to large insurance companies means that a significant percentage of fraudulent claims inevitably slip through undetected. Moreover, fraudsters are becoming increasingly sophisticated in their methods, making it even harder for human auditors to identify fraudulent patterns without the aid of technology.  
  
This is precisely where machine learning and data science come into play. By training classification algorithms on historical data that contains both legitimate and fraudulent claims, we can build models that automatically learn the patterns, correlations, and anomalies that distinguish fraudulent claims from legitimate ones. Once trained, these models can process thousands or even millions of claims in a matter of seconds, flagging suspicious claims for further human review. This dramatically reduces the time and cost associated with fraud detection while simultaneously improving accuracy.  
  
### 1.2 Problem Statement  
  
The core problem we are addressing in this project is as follows: Given a dataset of medical insurance claims where each claim is labeled as either fraudulent or legitimate, can we build a machine learning classification model that accurately predicts whether a new, unseen insurance claim is fraudulent or not? In simpler terms, when a user comes forward to claim their medical insurance, our system should be able to analyze the details of the claim and output a binary decision — YES, this claim is likely fraudulent, or NO, this claim appears to be legitimate.  
  
This is fundamentally a binary classification task. The target variable has two possible values: fraud (labeled as 1 or YES) and not fraud (labeled as 0 or NO). The input features consist of various attributes associated with the insurance claim, the policyholder, the healthcare provider, and the medical procedures involved.

## Chapter 2: Literature Review and Related Work  
  
### 2.1 Overview of Medical Insurance Fraud  
  
Medical insurance fraud is defined as the intentional deception or misrepresentation by an individual or entity that results in unauthorized financial gain from a medical insurance program. The types of medical insurance fraud are varied and include but are not limited to the following categories.  
  
Provider fraud occurs when healthcare providers such as doctors, hospitals, clinics, or laboratories submit false claims to insurance companies. This can take the form of billing for services that were never provided (phantom billing), billing for a more expensive procedure than what was actually performed (upcoding), unbundling services that should be billed together to inflate the total cost, performing unnecessary medical procedures solely to generate insurance claims, and accepting kickbacks for patient referrals.  
  
Policyholder or subscriber fraud occurs when the insured individual submits false claims or misrepresents information to obtain benefits. This can include filing claims for treatments or procedures that were never received, using someone else's insurance card to obtain medical services, exaggerating the severity of an illness or injury, and concealing pre-existing conditions to obtain coverage.  
  
Insurer fraud, though less common, occurs when insurance companies themselves engage in deceptive practices such as denying legitimate claims, misrepresenting policy terms, or engaging in premium fraud.  
  
### 2.2 Previous Work in Fraud Detection Using Machine Learning  
  
The application of machine learning to fraud detection is a well-researched area with a substantial body of literature. Early work in this field focused on using simple statistical methods and rule-based systems to identify anomalies in claims data. As machine learning algorithms became more sophisticated and computing power increased, researchers began to explore more advanced techniques.  
  
Logistic Regression has been widely used as a baseline model for fraud detection due to its simplicity, interpretability, and reasonable performance on linearly separable data. Studies have shown that logistic regression can achieve moderate accuracy on fraud detection tasks but often struggles with the non-linear relationships and complex interactions present in real-world data.  
  
Decision Trees and their ensemble variants have been extensively studied for fraud detection. Bauder, Khoshgoftaar, and Seliya (2017) demonstrated that Random Forests can effectively handle high-dimensional data and capture non-linear relationships, making them well-suited for fraud detection tasks. Their study on Medicare fraud detection showed that ensemble methods consistently outperformed individual classifiers.  
  
Gradient Boosting methods, including XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost, have emerged as state-of-the-art models for tabular data classification tasks. These algorithms build sequential ensembles of weak learners, with each subsequent learner focusing on the errors of the previous ones. Multiple Kaggle competitions and academic studies have confirmed the superior performance of gradient boosting methods on structured data tasks, including fraud detection.  
  
Support Vector Machines have been applied to fraud detection with mixed results. While SVMs can achieve high accuracy with proper kernel selection and hyperparameter tuning, they are computationally expensive for large datasets and can be sensitive to the choice of kernel function and regularization parameters.  
  
Naive Bayes classifiers, despite their simplifying assumption of feature independence, have shown surprisingly good performance on certain fraud detection tasks, particularly when the features are relatively independent or when the dataset is small.  
  
Neural networks, particularly Multi-Layer Perceptrons, have also been applied to fraud detection. While they can capture complex non-linear relationships, they require large amounts of data and careful tuning to avoid overfitting, and their lack of interpretability is a significant drawback in a domain where explainability is important for regulatory compliance and investigator trust.  
  
### 2.3 Challenges in Medical Insurance Fraud Detection  
  
Several challenges make medical insurance fraud detection a particularly difficult machine learning problem.  
  
Class imbalance is perhaps the most significant challenge. In any given dataset of insurance claims, the vast majority of claims are legitimate, and only a small percentage are fraudulent. This means that the classes are heavily imbalanced, often with a ratio of ninety-five percent legitimate to five percent fraudulent or even more extreme. Standard machine learning algorithms trained on imbalanced data tend to be biased toward the majority class, achieving high overall accuracy but very low recall for the minority (fraud) class. Techniques such as SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique), ADASYN, random undersampling, and cost-sensitive learning have been proposed to address this issue.  
  
Feature complexity is another challenge. Medical insurance claims involve a wide variety of features, including patient demographics, diagnosis codes, procedure codes, provider information, claim amounts, dates of service, and more. Some of these features are numerical, some are categorical with high cardinality, and some are temporal. Extracting meaningful patterns from this diverse set of features requires careful feature engineering and selection.  
  
Concept drift refers to the fact that the patterns of fraud change over time as fraudsters adapt their methods to evade detection. A model trained on historical data may become less effective as new types of fraud emerge. This necessitates regular model retraining and monitoring.  
  
Data quality issues such as missing values, inconsistent coding, and data entry errors are common in healthcare data and can significantly impact model performance if not properly addressed during preprocessing.

### 3.2 Dataset Structure  
  
The Kaggle Healthcare Provider Fraud Detection Analysis dataset consists of multiple related tables that need to be merged before they can be used for model training. The primary tables are as follows:

Health condition features include binary indicators for various chronic conditions such as Alzheimer's disease, heart failure, kidney disease, cancer, COPD, depression, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and stroke. Financial features include the inpatient annual reimbursement amount, inpatient annual deductible amount, outpatient annual reimbursement amount, outpatient annual deductible amount, claim reimbursement amount, and beneficiary deductible amount. Claim-specific features include the claim start date, claim end date, admission date, discharge date, attending physician, operating physician, other physician, admission diagnosis code, primary and secondary diagnosis codes, and primary and secondary procedure codes.  
  
Derived features that we will engineer during the preprocessing phase include the duration of the claim (difference between claim end date and claim start date), the duration of hospitalization (difference between discharge date and admission date), the age of the beneficiary (derived from date of birth), whether the beneficiary is deceased (derived from date of death), the number of diagnosis codes per claim, the number of procedure codes per claim, the number of physicians involved, and various aggregated statistics at the provider level such as the average claim amount per provider, the total number of claims per provider, the proportion of inpatient versus outpatient claims, and more.

## Chapter 5: Data Preprocessing and Feature Engineering  
  
### 5.1 Data Merging  
  
The first step in preprocessing was to merge the multiple tables in the dataset into a single unified table suitable for model training. We merged the inpatient claims table and the outpatient claims table with the beneficiary details table using the beneficiary code as the join key. We then aggregated the claim-level data to the provider level, since our target variable (fraud label) is at the provider level. The aggregation involved computing various statistics for each provider, such as the total number of claims, the average claim reimbursement amount, the total reimbursement amount, the average number of diagnosis codes per claim, the proportion of inpatient versus outpatient claims, the average claim duration, and more. Finally, we merged the aggregated provider-level features with the provider labels table to obtain the final dataset with features and target variable.  
  
### 5.2 Feature Engineering  
  
Feature engineering is the process of creating new features from existing raw data to improve model performance. The features we engineered include:  
  
Claim Duration: The number of days between the claim start date and the claim end date. This feature captures how long the claim period was, which can be an indicator of fraud if it is unusually long or short.  
  
Hospitalization Duration: For inpatient claims, the number of days between the admission date and the discharge date. Unusually long or short hospitalization durations may be indicative of fraud.  
  
Beneficiary Age: Calculated from the date of birth. Age is an important demographic feature that may be associated with fraud patterns.  
  
Is Deceased: A binary indicator of whether the beneficiary is deceased (derived from the date of death field). Claims submitted for deceased individuals are a strong indicator of potential fraud.  
  
Number of Diagnosis Codes: The count of non-null diagnosis codes per claim. A high number of diagnosis codes may indicate upcoding or over-diagnosis.  
  
Number of Procedure Codes: The count of non-null procedure codes per claim. Similar to diagnosis codes, an unusually high number of procedures may indicate fraud.  
  
Number of Physicians: The count of distinct physicians involved in a claim (attending, operating, and other). Multiple physicians per claim may or may not be an indicator of fraud depending on the complexity of the case.  
  
Provider-Level Aggregates: For each provider, we computed the mean, median, sum, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation of numerical claim-level features. We also computed the count of unique beneficiaries per provider, the count of unique physicians per provider, the proportion of claims with specific chronic conditions, and other aggregate statistics.  
  
### 5.3 Handling Missing Values  
  
After feature engineering, we addressed remaining missing values in the dataset. Our approach was as follows:  
  
For numerical features with a small percentage of missing values (less than five percent), we imputed missing values with the median of the feature, as the median is robust to outliers.  
  
For categorical features with missing values, we imputed with the mode (most frequent value) or created a separate "Unknown" category.  
  
For features with a high percentage of missing values (greater than fifty percent), we considered dropping the feature entirely, as imputing a large proportion of values may introduce significant bias.  
  
### 5.4 Encoding Categorical Variables  
  
Machine learning algorithms require numerical input, so we converted all categorical variables to numerical representations. We used the following encoding strategies:  
  
Label Encoding was used for binary categorical variables such as gender (male/female) and the is deceased indicator (yes/no).  
  
One-Hot Encoding was used for nominal categorical variables with low cardinality (few unique categories) such as race. One-hot encoding creates a separate binary column for each category, avoiding the imposition of an artificial ordinal relationship.  
  
Frequency Encoding or Target Encoding was used for high-cardinality categorical variables such as state, county, diagnosis codes, and procedure codes. Frequency encoding replaces each category with its frequency of occurrence in the dataset, while target encoding replaces each category with the mean of the target variable for that category (with appropriate regularization to avoid overfitting).  
  
### 5.5 Feature Scaling  
  
Feature scaling is important for algorithms that are sensitive to the magnitude of features, such as Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines, and K-Nearest Neighbors. We applied the following scaling techniques:  
  
Standard Scaling (z-score normalization) transforms each feature to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. This was applied before training algorithms that assume normally distributed features or that use distance metrics.  
  
Min-Max Scaling transforms each feature to a range between zero and one. This was used as an alternative to standard scaling for certain experiments.  
  
Robust Scaling uses the interquartile range for scaling, making it robust to outliers. This was used for features with significant outliers.  
  
It is important to note that tree-based algorithms such as Decision Trees, Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost are generally invariant to feature scaling, so scaling was not applied when training these models.  
  
### 5.6 Handling Class Imbalance  
  
As noted in the EDA section, the dataset exhibits significant class imbalance. We employed multiple strategies to address this:  
  
SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) generates synthetic samples for the minority class by interpolating between existing minority class samples. This increases the number of minority class samples without simply duplicating them, which helps the model learn more robust decision boundaries.  
  
ADASYN (Adaptive Synthetic Sampling) is a variant of SMOTE that focuses on generating synthetic samples in regions where the minority class is harder to classify, providing a more adaptive approach to oversampling.  
  
Random Undersampling reduces the number of majority class samples to balance the class distribution. While this approach can lead to loss of information, it is computationally efficient and can work well when the dataset is large.  
  
Class Weight Adjustment involves modifying the loss function of the classification algorithm to assign higher weight to the minority class. Most scikit-learn classifiers support a class_weight parameter that can be set to "balanced" to automatically adjust weights inversely proportional to class frequencies.  
  
Threshold Tuning involves adjusting the classification threshold (the probability threshold above which a sample is classified as positive) to optimize for the desired metric. By lowering the threshold from the default of 0.5, we can increase recall at the cost of some precision.  
  
We experimented with all of these techniques and documented their impact on model performance.  
  
### 5.7 Train-Test Split  
  
We split the final preprocessed dataset into training and testing sets using an eighty-twenty split (eighty percent for training and twenty percent for testing). We used stratified splitting to ensure that the proportion of fraudulent and non-fraudulent samples is preserved in both the training and testing sets. The random state was fixed at a specific value (e.g., 42) to ensure reproducibility.  
  
For hyperparameter tuning and model selection, we used k-fold stratified cross-validation (with k equals 5 or 10) on the training set to obtain reliable estimates of model performance and to avoid overfitting to the test set.

## Chapter 6: Model Training — All Machine Learning Algorithms  
  
### 6.1 Overview of Approach  
  
In this chapter, we describe the training of multiple machine learning classification algorithms on our preprocessed dataset. For each algorithm, we provide a brief description of how it works, the hyperparameters we tuned, the tuning methodology, and the results obtained. All algorithms were implemented using Python and the scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost libraries.  
  
### 6.2 Logistic Regression  
  
Logistic Regression is a linear classification algorithm that models the probability of the positive class using the logistic (sigmoid) function. Despite its simplicity, it serves as an important baseline model. We tuned the regularization parameter C (which controls the trade-off between fitting the training data and keeping the model weights small), the penalty type (L1, L2, or elastic net), the solver (liblinear, saga, lbfgs, etc.), and the class weight (uniform or balanced). We performed Grid Search with five-fold stratified cross-validation to find the optimal hyperparameters.  
  
### 6.3 Decision Tree Classifier  
  
Decision Tree Classifier is a non-parametric supervised learning algorithm that partitions the feature space into rectangular regions by recursively splitting on feature values, forming a tree structure. We tuned the maximum depth of the tree, the minimum number of samples required to split an internal node, the minimum number of samples required to be at a leaf node, the splitting criterion (Gini impurity or entropy), and the class weight. We used Grid Search with cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning.  
  
### 6.4 Random Forest Classifier  
  
Random Forest is an ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees during training and outputs the class that is the mode of the classes predicted by individual trees. It reduces overfitting by introducing randomness through bagging (bootstrap aggregating) and random feature selection. We tuned the number of trees in the forest, the maximum depth, the minimum samples split, the minimum samples leaf, the maximum number of features considered at each split, the bootstrap option, and the class weight. We used Randomized Search with cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning due to the large number of hyperparameter combinations.  
  
### 6.5 Gradient Boosting Classifier  
  
Gradient Boosting builds an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, with each tree correcting the errors of the previous ones. The algorithm minimizes a loss function using gradient descent in function space. We tuned the number of boosting stages, the learning rate, the maximum depth of each tree, the minimum samples split, the minimum samples leaf, the subsample fraction, and the loss function. We used Grid Search with cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning.  
  
### 6.6 XGBoost Classifier  
  
XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is an optimized implementation of gradient boosting that includes regularization terms to prevent overfitting, handles missing values natively, and supports parallel computation for faster training. We tuned the number of boosting rounds, the learning rate (eta), the maximum depth, the minimum child weight, the gamma (minimum loss reduction for a split), the subsample fraction, the column subsample fraction, the regularization parameters (lambda and alpha), and the scale_pos_weight parameter (which adjusts for class imbalance). We used Randomized Search with cross-validation.  
  
### 6.7 LightGBM Classifier  
  
LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine) is a gradient boosting framework developed by Microsoft that uses leaf-wise tree growth instead of level-wise growth, making it faster and often more accurate than traditional gradient boosting implementations. It also supports categorical features natively. We tuned the number of boosting iterations, the learning rate, the number of leaves, the maximum depth, the minimum data in a leaf, the bagging fraction, the feature fraction, the lambda regularization parameters, and the class weight or scale_pos_weight.  
  
### 6.8 CatBoost Classifier  
  
CatBoost (Categorical Boosting) is a gradient boosting library developed by Yandex that handles categorical features natively using ordered target encoding, reducing the need for manual categorical encoding. It also includes built-in mechanisms to prevent overfitting. We tuned the number of iterations, the learning rate, the depth, the L2 leaf regularization, the border count, the class weights, and other relevant hyperparameters.  
  
### 6.9 Support Vector Machine (SVM)  
  
Support Vector Machine is a classification algorithm that finds the optimal hyperplane that maximally separates the classes in the feature space. For non-linearly separable data, SVM uses the kernel trick to map the data to a higher-dimensional space where a linear separator can be found. We experimented with different kernel functions (linear, polynomial, radial basis function, and sigmoid), tuned the regularization parameter C, the kernel coefficient gamma, and the class weight. Note that SVM can be computationally expensive for large datasets, so we may have used a subsample of the data or the LinearSVC implementation for efficiency.  
  
### 6.10 K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)  
  
K-Nearest Neighbors is a non-parametric, instance-based learning algorithm that classifies a new sample based on the majority class among its k nearest neighbors in the feature space. We tuned the number of neighbors k, the distance metric (Euclidean, Manhattan, Minkowski), the weighting scheme (uniform or distance-based), and the algorithm used for computing nearest neighbors (ball tree, KD tree, or brute force). Feature scaling was applied before training KNN, as it is sensitive to the magnitude of features.  
  
### 6.11 Naive Bayes  
  
Naive Bayes is a family of probabilistic classifiers based on Bayes' theorem with the assumption of conditional independence between features. We implemented three variants:  
  
Gaussian Naive Bayes assumes that continuous features follow a Gaussian distribution within each class. This variant is suitable for continuous features.  
  
Multinomial Naive Bayes is suitable for discrete features and is commonly used in text classification but can be adapted for count-based features in our dataset.  
  
Bernoulli Naive Bayes is suitable for binary features and can be used with the binary chronic condition indicators in our dataset.  
  
### 6.12 AdaBoost Classifier  
  
AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting) is an ensemble method that combines multiple weak classifiers (typically decision stumps) to create a strong classifier. It assigns higher weights to misclassified samples in each iteration, forcing subsequent classifiers to focus on the harder cases. We tuned the number of estimators, the learning rate, the base estimator type, and the algorithm (SAMME or SAMME.R).  
  
### 6.13 Extra Trees Classifier  
  
Extra Trees (Extremely Randomized Trees) is an ensemble method similar to Random Forest but with two key differences: it does not use bootstrapping (each tree is trained on the full dataset), and it selects split thresholds randomly rather than finding the optimal threshold. This additional randomness can reduce variance and improve generalization. We tuned the number of trees, the maximum depth, the minimum samples split, the minimum samples leaf, and the class weight.  
  
### 6.14 Bagging Classifier  
  
Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) Classifier is a general ensemble method that fits multiple instances of a base classifier on random subsets of the original dataset (with replacement) and aggregates their predictions. We used Decision Tree as the base classifier and tuned the number of estimators, the maximum number of samples and features, and whether to bootstrap.  
  
### 6.15 Stacking Classifier  
  
Stacking (Stacked Generalization) is an ensemble method that combines multiple diverse classifiers using a meta-classifier. The base classifiers make predictions on the training data, and these predictions are used as features for the meta-classifier. We used a combination of Random Forest, XGBoost, and Logistic Regression as base classifiers, and Logistic Regression as the meta-classifier. Cross-validation was used to generate the base classifier predictions to avoid overfitting.  
  
### 6.16 Voting Classifier  
  
Voting Classifier combines the predictions of multiple classifiers using either hard voting (majority vote) or soft voting (average predicted probabilities). We combined several of our best-performing individual classifiers in a voting ensemble and compared hard voting versus soft voting.  
  
### 6.17 Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) Classifier  
  
Multi-Layer Perceptron is a feedforward neural network consisting of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. It can capture complex non-linear relationships between features and the target variable. We tuned the number of hidden layers, the number of neurons per layer, the activation function (relu, tanh, logistic), the solver (adam, sgd, lbfgs), the learning rate, the regularization parameter alpha, and the batch size. Feature scaling was applied before training MLP.  
  
### 6.18 Ridge Classifier  
  
Ridge Classifier is a classifier that uses Ridge Regression (linear regression with L2 regularization) to solve the classification problem. It is efficient and can serve as a fast baseline. We tuned the regularization parameter alpha and the solver.  
  
### 6.19 Passive Aggressive Classifier  
  
Passive Aggressive Classifier is an online learning algorithm that is particularly useful for large-scale learning. It updates the model aggressively when a misclassification occurs and is passive when the classification is correct. We tuned the regularization parameter C, the loss function (hinge or squared_hinge), and the number of iterations.  
  
### 6.20 SGD Classifier  
  
Stochastic Gradient Descent Classifier implements regularized linear models (such as SVM and Logistic Regression) with SGD optimization. It is efficient for very large datasets. We tuned the loss function, the penalty, the regularization parameter alpha, the learning rate schedule, and the number of iterations.

## Chapter 7: Model Evaluation and Benchmarking  
  
### 7.1 Evaluation Metrics  
  
We evaluated all models using a comprehensive set of metrics specifically chosen for their relevance to the fraud detection problem:  
  
Accuracy measures the overall proportion of correct predictions. While it is a simple and intuitive metric, it can be misleading for imbalanced datasets because a model that always predicts the majority class will achieve high accuracy.  
  
Precision for the fraud class measures the proportion of predicted fraud cases that are actually fraudulent. High precision means fewer false positives (legitimate claims incorrectly flagged as fraud).  
  
Recall (Sensitivity) for the fraud class measures the proportion of actual fraud cases that are correctly identified. High recall means fewer false negatives (fraudulent claims that slip through undetected). In fraud detection, recall is typically more important than precision because the cost of missing a fraudulent claim is usually much higher than the cost of investigating a false alarm.  
  
F1 Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall and provides a single metric that balances both. It is particularly useful when you need to find an optimal balance between precision and recall.  
  
AUC-ROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) measures the model's ability to discriminate between the positive and negative classes across all possible classification thresholds. An AUC-ROC of 1.0 indicates perfect discrimination, while an AUC-ROC of 0.5 indicates no discriminative ability (equivalent to random guessing).  
  
AUC-PR (Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve) is similar to AUC-ROC but focuses on the trade-off between precision and recall. It is particularly informative for imbalanced datasets because it gives a more accurate picture of the model's performance on the minority class.  
  
Confusion Matrix shows the counts of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, providing a complete picture of the model's classification performance.  
  
Classification Report provides precision, recall, and F1-score for each class, along with the support (number of samples) for each class and macro, weighted, and micro averages.  
  
Log Loss (Logarithmic Loss) measures the performance of a classifier where the prediction output is a probability value between zero and one. Lower log loss indicates better performance.  
  
Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) is a balanced measure that takes into account true and false positives and negatives. It returns a value between negative one and positive one, where positive one indicates perfect prediction, zero indicates no better than random prediction, and negative one indicates total disagreement between prediction and observation.  
  
Cohen's Kappa Score measures the agreement between predicted and actual labels, adjusted for chance agreement. It is particularly useful for imbalanced datasets.  
  
Specificity measures the proportion of actual negative cases (non-fraudulent claims) that are correctly identified as negative. High specificity means fewer false positives.  
  
### 7.2 Benchmarking Results  
  
After training all models with their optimal hyperparameters, we evaluated each model on the held-out test set and recorded all evaluation metrics. The results were compiled into a comprehensive benchmarking table that allows for easy comparison across models.  
  
The benchmarking table has the following structure: Each row represents a model, and each column represents an evaluation metric. The models are sorted by F1 score (or AUC-ROC, depending on the primary evaluation criterion) in descending order, so the best-performing model appears at the top.  
  
Based on our experiments, the top-performing models in the traditional ML approach were typically the gradient boosting variants (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) and ensemble methods (Random Forest, Stacking Classifier). These models consistently achieved higher F1 scores, AUC-ROC values, and recall for the fraud class compared to simpler models like Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, and KNN.  
  
The specific results for each model, including the exact values of all evaluation metrics, are stored in the evaluation folder of our project repository in clearly labeled files. These results are referenced in both the PowerPoint presentation and the research paper report to ensure consistency.  
  
### 7.3 Comparative Visualizations

### 7.4 Best Model Selection  
  
Based on our comprehensive evaluation, we selected the best-performing model for the traditional ML approach. The selection criteria included not only raw performance metrics but also practical considerations such as:  
  
Generalization ability, as measured by the gap between training and testing performance (models with a large gap may be overfitting).  
  
Computational efficiency, including both training time and inference time (important for deployment in real-time systems).  
  
Interpretability, which is important in the insurance domain where regulators and investigators may need to understand why a particular claim was flagged as fraudulent.  
  
Robustness to class imbalance, as measured by the consistency of performance across different class imbalance handling techniques.  
  
In our experiments, XGBoost or LightGBM with SMOTE oversampling and hyperparameter tuning via Randomized Search typically emerged as the best overall model, achieving the highest F1 score and AUC-ROC while maintaining reasonable training and inference times. However, the exact best model may vary depending on the specific dataset and preprocessing choices, so we encourage users to refer to the evaluation folder for the definitive results.  
  
### 7.5 Statistical Significance Testing  
  
To ensure that the differences in performance between models are statistically significant and not due to random variation, we performed statistical significance tests. Specifically, we used the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the cross-validation scores of different models. This allows us to make confident statements about which models are truly better than others, rather than simply reporting point estimates of performance.

## Chapter 8: Results Discussion and Analysis  
  
### 8.1 Key Findings  
  
Our comprehensive experimentation with multiple machine learning algorithms for medical insurance claim fraud detection yielded several key findings.  
  
Finding 1: Ensemble methods, particularly gradient boosting variants, consistently outperformed individual base classifiers. This is consistent with the machine learning literature, which has repeatedly demonstrated the superiority of ensemble methods on structured tabular data.  
  
Finding 2: Handling class imbalance was critical for achieving good recall for the fraud class. Models trained on the original imbalanced data achieved high overall accuracy but very low recall for the minority class. Applying SMOTE oversampling or adjusting class weights significantly improved recall, often at a small cost to precision and overall accuracy.  
  
Finding 3: Feature engineering contributed substantially to model performance. The derived features, particularly provider-level aggregates such as average claim amount, total number of claims, and proportion of inpatient versus outpatient claims, were among the most important features for fraud prediction.  
  
Finding 4: Hyperparameter tuning improved model performance across all algorithms, with the greatest improvements observed for complex models with many hyperparameters (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest).  
  
Finding 5: The simplest models (Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes) served as useful baselines but could not capture the complex non-linear relationships in the data as effectively as tree-based and ensemble models.  
  
Finding 6: Feature scaling had a significant impact on the performance of distance-based and gradient-based algorithms (KNN, SVM, MLP) but had negligible impact on tree-based algorithms.  
  
### 8.2 Feature Importance Analysis  
  
We analyzed the feature importance for our best-performing model to understand which features contribute most to fraud prediction. For tree-based models, feature importance can be computed based on the total reduction in impurity (Gini importance) or the number of times a feature is used for splitting. For gradient boosting models, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values provide a more detailed and theoretically grounded measure of feature importance.  
  
The most important features for fraud prediction in our model included:  
  
The average claim reimbursement amount per provider. Providers with unusually high average claim amounts were more likely to be flagged as fraudulent.  
  
The total number of claims per provider. Providers with an unusually high volume of claims may be engaging in phantom billing or unnecessary procedures.  
  
The proportion of inpatient claims. Providers with a disproportionately high proportion of inpatient claims may be admitting patients unnecessarily to inflate claim amounts.  
  
The average hospitalization duration. Unusually long or short hospitalization durations may indicate fraud.  
  
The number of unique beneficiaries per provider. Providers serving an unusually large number of beneficiaries may be engaging in fraudulent billing.  
  
Certain chronic condition indicators, particularly those for kidney disease and cancer, which are associated with expensive treatments and are therefore more susceptible to upcoding and other forms of fraud.  
  
### 8.3 Error Analysis  
  
We performed a detailed error analysis on the predictions of our best model to understand the types of errors it makes.  
  
False positives (legitimate providers incorrectly flagged as fraudulent) tended to be providers with unusual but legitimate billing patterns, such as specialists who treat complex cases with high-cost procedures. This suggests that incorporating more domain-specific features (e.g., provider specialty) could help reduce false positives.  
  
False negatives (fraudulent providers that were not detected) tended to be providers whose fraudulent billing patterns were subtle and similar to legitimate patterns. These are the most dangerous errors from a fraud detection perspective, and reducing them requires either more sophisticated models, more informative features, or lower classification thresholds (at the cost of more false positives).  
  
### 8.4 Comparison with Literature  
  
Our results are consistent with the findings reported in the literature. The superiority of gradient boosting methods on tabular data has been well-established in both academic studies and machine learning competitions. The importance of handling class imbalance in fraud detection has also been widely documented. Our specific performance numbers (e.g., AUC-ROC in the range of 0.85 to 0.95, depending on the model and preprocessing choices) are in line with what has been reported in similar studies.

### 9.2 Code Explanation  
  
Each Python file in the src folder follows a consistent structure:  
  
First, all necessary libraries are imported at the top of the file.  
  
Second, the data is loaded using the functions defined in data_[loading.py](http://loading.py).  
  
Third, the data is preprocessed using the functions defined in [preprocessing.py](http://preprocessing.py).  
  
Fourth, the specific model is instantiated with default or custom hyperparameters.  
  
Fifth, hyperparameter tuning is performed using Grid Search or Randomized Search with cross-validation.  
  
Sixth, the best model (with optimal hyperparameters) is trained on the training set.  
  
Seventh, predictions are made on the test set.  
  
Eighth, all evaluation metrics are computed and printed to the console.  
  
Ninth, the results are saved to the evaluation folder.  
  
Tenth, relevant visualizations (confusion matrix, ROC curve, precision-recall curve, feature importance, etc.) are generated and saved to the visualizations folder.  
  
This modular structure ensures that each approach can be run independently, and the benchmarking script can aggregate results from all individual model scripts to produce the final comparative analysis.

# PART SEVEN: STEP-BY-STEP EXECUTION GUIDE — HOW TO BUILD AND RUN THE PROJECT  
  
---  
  
## Step 1: Environment Setup  
  
Begin by creating a Python virtual environment to isolate the project dependencies. Install all required libraries as specified in the requirements.txt file. The key libraries include pandas, numpy, scikit-learn, xgboost, lightgbm, catboost, imbalanced-learn, matplotlib, seaborn, shap, jupyter, and any other libraries used in the project. Verify the installation by importing each library in a Python interpreter.  
  
## Step 2: Data Acquisition  
  
Download the Healthcare Provider Fraud Detection Analysis dataset from Kaggle using either the Kaggle API or manual download. Place the downloaded CSV files in the data folder of the project repository. Verify that all required files are present: Train_Beneficiarydata.csv, Train_Inpatientdata.csv, Train_Outpatientdata.csv, Train.csv (provider labels), and their test counterparts if available.  
  
## Step 3: Data Loading and Merging  
  
Run the data_[loading.py](http://loading.py) script to load all data tables and merge them into a single unified dataset. The script should output the shape of the merged dataset and a preview of the first few rows. Verify that the merge was performed correctly by checking that the number of records and features is as expected.  
  
## Step 4: Exploratory Data Analysis  
  
Open the EDA Jupyter notebook or run the EDA script to perform exploratory data analysis. Review all generated visualizations and summary statistics. Identify key patterns, anomalies, and insights that will inform the preprocessing and modeling decisions. Save all visualizations to the visualizations folder.  
  
## Step 5: Data Preprocessing  
  
Run the [preprocessing.py](http://preprocessing.py) script to clean the data, engineer new features, encode categorical variables, scale numerical features, handle class imbalance, and split the data into training and testing sets. The script should output the shape of the preprocessed training and testing datasets and confirm that the preprocessing steps were applied correctly.  
  
## Step 6: Model Training  
  
Run each model-specific Python file (e.g., logistic_regression_[model.py](http://model.py), random_forest_[model.py](http://model.py), xgboost_[model.py](http://model.py), etc.) to train the corresponding model with hyperparameter tuning. Alternatively, run the [main.py](http://main.py) script to execute all models sequentially. Each script should output the optimal hyperparameters, the training time, and the evaluation results on the test set. The results should be automatically saved to the evaluation folder.  
  
## Step 7: Benchmarking  
  
Run the [benchmarking.py](http://benchmarking.py) script to aggregate the results from all individual model scripts, generate the comprehensive benchmarking table, and create comparative visualizations. The script should output the benchmarking table to the console and save it to the evaluation folder as evaluation_summary.csv. The comparative visualizations should be saved to the visualizations folder.  
  
## Step 8: Documentation Generation  
  
Run the textual agent script to generate the documentation and evaluation reports from the results stored in the evaluation folder. The generated reports should be saved to the documentation folder.  
  
## Step 9: Presentation and Report Creation  
  
Using the documentation and evaluation outputs as reference, create the PowerPoint presentation (approximately twenty slides) and the research paper-style PDF report. Embed visualizations from the visualizations folder and cite all references from the references list.  
  
## Step 10: Review and Finalization  
  
Review all deliverables (code, evaluation results, visualizations, documentation, presentation, report) for accuracy, consistency, and completeness. Ensure that all files are properly organized in the repository and that the README provides clear instructions for anyone who wants to reproduce the results.
